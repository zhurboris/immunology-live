AUTOIMMUNE DISORDERS DUE TO DEFICIENCY OF SUPPRESSION

H. Fudenberg (1971) has originally coined an interpretation of autoallergy as “immunodeficiency of suppression”. According to later concepts, the key role in autoreactive clone suppression belongs to recognition of these clone idiotypes by suppressing cells with subsequent antiidiotypic response via autoantibodies or T-suppressor factors (M. McNamara, H. Koler; 1983).

An important role in failure of self-tolerance mechanisms thus may be taken by T-, B-, and combined immunodeficits. In all these disorders indirect suppression of autoreactive B-cells can be altered or disabled due to incomplete repertoire of idiotype-antiidiotypic interaction, or owing to decrease in suppressor’s number and activity (see above “ B-lymphocyte and plasma cell biology”).

The direct antigen-specific suppressing of autoreactive B-clones is not possible, because clonal deletion and clonal anergy mechanisms, normally, withdraw or keep silent not only autoreactive T-helpers, but also the sources of direct autoreactive suppressor influences. So, the stimulation of autoreactive B-cells (by mitogens, by superantigens and other polyclonal activators) is normally counterbalanced mostly by indirect suppressing. If this mechanism would fall out – the activation tendency may predominate and result in autoallergy.

This particular mechanism is account for murine SLE models and is revealed also in SLE patients. Some authors also blame it for autoimmune components of AIDS pathogenesis (A. Kuhlberg, 1989; J. Kennedy, 1990). There is an age-associated loss of T-supressor cells in the NZB+NZW (F1) mice, which develop an autoimmune disease similar to SLE as they age.

Autoallergic manifestations accompany many other immunodeficiency syndromes. For example, in Bruton’s X-linked agammaglobulinemia some common symptoms are polyarthritis, periarteritis and Krohn’s disease. Break of self-tolerance and autoallergy are observed in different kinds of thymic pathology (neonatal hypoplasia or aplasia of thymus, thymectomy, irradiation of thymic gland, hypercortisolism). As the thymic gland a site of T-cell differentiation, such disorders alter significantly T-dependent components of immune cooperation and provoke immunodeficit. On the background of T-cell immunodeficiency, there are manifestations of self-tolerance break. It could be expressed in bright systemic autoallergy, but as the process takes place in parallel with severe immunodeficiency, its autoimmune symptoms are weakened. Moreover, various infectious and oncological sequels of immunodeficit mask them.

Besides total failure of self-tolerance mechanisms, selective loss of tolerance towards one or few autoantigens is possible.

The reason for it is, mostly often, hereditary (or sometimes acquired) defect in presentation of one (or some) concrete autoantigens by thymic D-cells. There is no elimination or intrathymic suppression of the appropriate autoreactive T-clone(s), so they may be later activated and, in turn, cause certain autoreactive B-clone(s) activation in peripheral organs. The disorders of this kind usually cause severe familiar organospecific autoallergic disorders, like familiar form of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (R. Cesareo et al. 1997), related to autosomal dominant trait of intolerance to self thyroid peroxidase epitope. The greater incidence of autoallergic disorders among women is also explained by some authors, as a result of suppressor activity decrease under the influence of estrogens (N. Talal, 1983). I. Roitt in articles dated back to 1984 even hypothesized the existence of special subset of contra-suppressor lymphocytes, whose activation may facilitate autoallergy. But later this concept was not confirmed experimentally.

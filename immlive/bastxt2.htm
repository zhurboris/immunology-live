Mucosal Immunity

The Regional Lymphoid Systems Associated with the Mucosa (RLSAM) are presented with multiple non-encapsulated lymphoid elements, residing in lamina propria of the mucous membranes and in submucosal layer. RLSAM are covered with basal membranes and superficial epithelia, which contain mucous and other glands and secretory goblet cells. The crossing point of gut and respiratory tract is a place of extensive and broad antigenic stimulation. Non-occasionally, it is the location of Waldeier-Pirogov ring, which consists of such RLSAM elements: tonsils. These are often involved in inflammation in sore throat (tonsillitis). There are palatial, nasopharyngeal, laryngeal, and tongue tonsils. The trachea, ENT sinuses and bronchi also have RLSAM elements. RLSAM of respiratory mucosa includes also lymphoid elements of terminal respiratory tracts and chylus lymph nodes. Respiratory RLSAM is responsible for synthesis and secretion of Immunoglobulin A (IgA) during immune reactions provoked by inhaled antigens. Local lymphoid elements exist within lamina propria and submucosa of urogenital tracts as well. Gut associated lymphoid tissue is quite abundant and includes intestinal units – Peyer’s patches and lymphoid elements of the appendix. Polyclonal immunostimulation of appendix lymphoid cells with certain antigens (for example, that of Yersenia) may cause the clinical picture of acute appendicitis. The hepatic lymphoid cells cooperate with local macrophages (totally representing in rodents about 30 % of liver mass) and also belong to RLSAM.

These gut elements respond for ingested antigens and also are able to generate IgA.

Totally RLSAM is presented mostly with cytotoxic and helper T-cells (up to 70%). There are also less than 30% B-cells forming germinal follicles, mostly within lamina propria. Plasma cells of these compartments produce IgA. Submucosal layer contains almost exclusively T-cells. The specific of intraepithelial RLSAM T-cells is peculiar type of surface T-cell receptor. They belong to minor subset of T-lymphocytes with g s - type of antigenic receptors. It is believed that their specialization is related to immune surveillance over mutant cells and cells containing persistent intracellular pathogens.

Unlike other classes of antibodies, IgA are subdued to endocytosis into the cells of mucosa epithelium via special polyglobulin receptor. Within these non-lymphoid cells IgA join to special secretory component (of epithelial origin), which protects them from proteolysis during the process of transcytosis. Finally, IgA are excreted with mucus, bile or other secrets on the surface of mucosa or into the lumen of an organ (K. Mostoff, R. Blobel; 1982).

RLSAM is sequestered from other peripheral lymphoid organs due to its “intro-barrier” (A.S. Shevelyov, 1992) location. During immune responses it is able to react as a whole entity. Cause of this, local sensitization of the lymphocytes in one of RLSAM compartments some time later may be transfigured into systemic hypersensitivity and boosted immune response occurs through all its surface. Moreover, cross-reactions may occur with enteric rout of sensitization and boosted immune response over respiratory tract mucosa – for example in food allergy associated with bronchial asthma. These phenomena exist owing to unique routs of lymphocyte circulation and homing, characteristic for mucosa associated lymphoid tissue. The intestinal mucosa contains special antigen-transporting M-cells, which absorb the gut antigens, process them and fetch to lymphocytes. After contact with a gut antigen, many of local lymphocytes penetrate into systemic bloodstream and, later, return back home via intestinal HEVs. This is established by means of recognition of unique addressin molecules, expressed exclusively at the place of gut lymphocyte destination. Gut-associated lymphocytes are very essential for the development of tolerance towards enteric antigens. The lytic abilities of digestive system protect intestinal lymphocytes from excessive antigenic challenge. There is some kind of local balance on the mucosa membranes of gut, achieved between normal microflora and lymphocytes. This system stays in the state of symbiosis and does not display too strong immune responses against normal inhabitants of healthy man’s gut (status known as eubacteriosis). The breast-feeding facilitates the formation of such immune-bacterial balance. Under some pathologic conditions, digestion and decay of certain antigens and/or polyclonal lymphoid stimulators is not efficient or incomplete. Due to imbalance of antigenic environment, gut lymphocytes may respond with hypersensitivity or even autoallergic reactions.

A bright example is one of the mechanisms in juvenile sugar diabetes (insulin dependent Diabetes Mellitus – IDDM) pathogenesis.

The enteric immunization with some peptides derived from bovine serum (milk) albumin in artificial feeding of infants, especially, premature babies is risky for subsequent provocation of autoallergy against self-insulin. This is typical for the carriers of D3 and D4 HLA antigens, who poorly distinguish between self and alien peptides, and may even cause progressive autoimmune insulitis, b - cell insufficiency and IDDM onset (A. Karjalainen et al.; 1992). Non-digested cereal proteins - avenin and gliadin may be retained within gut mucosa and act as polyclonal lectin-like immunostimulants. They boost different local lymphocytes, including autoreactive ones. This mechanism is observed in chronic autoimmune atrophic enteritis of celiakia (sprue) patients. Cross-reaction of intestinal RLSAM between the antigens of non-pathogenic E. Coli and bowel mucosa may contribute into mechanisms of non-specific ulcerative colitis etc.

Regional Lymphoid System Associated With Skin

Similarly to RLSAM, it is possible to delineate regional lymphoid system associated with skin (RLSAS). The antigens, penetrating via skin, are engulfed and presented by epidermal Langerhans cells – a kind of skin APC. Langerhans cells are able to cooperate with the lymphocytes directly within the skin or after migration – into regional lymph nodes.

This compartment of immune system is of greatest importance for the development of contact dermatitis, tuberculin-like reactions, bullous pemphigus, psoriasis and many others immunopathological skin disease. Skin plasma cells are also able to produce IgA, which penetrates here into the discharge of sweat and sebaceous skin glands.

 

 

Blood

The blood itself is an constitutive part of the immune system. All lymphoid and non-lymphoid cell lines as well as their humoral products are presented within bloodstream .

Blood samples are commonly taken for clinical evaluation of immune system parameters (WBC total number and differential count, lymphoid subsets percentage, immunoglobulins level, circulating immune complexes concentration, cytokines content etc.). Sometimes the picture revealed in peripheral blood smear corresponds to the data, characteristic for other lymphoid organs. For example, in AIDS the same shifts in CD4 and CD8-positive lymphocytes equation are emblematic both for peripheral blood and bone marrow.

In sarcoidosis involved lung tissue and peripheral blood have different subset content of lymphocytes. In demyelinizing disorders of CNS (multiple sclerosis, lateral amyotrophic sclerosis, Guillen-Barr syndrome) blood lymphocytes and intrathecal lymphoid cells of cerebrospinal fluid are principally different in their activity and set of surface markers. The blood is an arena for “graft versus host” reactions. In mismatched blood transfusion it is the scene of “host versus graft” cytotoxic response.

Now we shall discuss the "cellular composition " of the immune response.

Antigen presenting cells (APC)

The system of mononuclear phagocytes and some closely related kinds of cells (glial, endothelial, and thymic stellate cells of ectodermal origin) basically, perform the function of the antigen-presentation.

.  

Macrophages are a part of the mononuclear phagocyte system, descendants of bone marrow promonocytes and peripheral blood monocytes. Macrophages leave bloodstream and emigrate in different tissues, where acquire some specific features and are called by different names (histiocytes, osteoclasts, alveolar, peritoneal, pleural macrophages, Kupffer’s cells etc.). However, having the same origin and occupation they all belong to the same dynasty of mononuclear phagocytes and play an important role in inflammation. Besides macrophages realize essential functions in the immune response.

A branch of this dynasty is represented by dendritic and Langerhans' cells.

This group includes a population of cells that are all characterized by dendritic cytoplasmic processes and the presence of large amounts of cell-surface class II HLA molecules. Dendritic cells are found in the lymphoid tissues, and somewhat similar cells within the epidermis have been called Langerhans' cells. By virtue of cell-surface class II molecules, dendritic cells and Langerhans' cells are extremely efficient in antigen presentation, and some investigators believe that they are the most important antigen-presenting cells in the body. It should be noted that although they share antigen-presenting capacity with macrophages, unlike the latter cell type they are weakly phagocytic.

The main antigen-processing cell for B-lymphocytes is dendritic reticular cell of lymphoid follicles. For T-lymphocytes it is interdigiting reticular cell of lymph node paracortical zone.

 

APC clones are not restricted with processing of certain unique antigen, but rather able to present any heteroantigens or autoantigens to lymphoid cells of appropriate clonal specificity. B cell themselves also may present antigens in association with MHC-proteins, but, unlike APC, it is clonally restricted with one antigen – that, recognized by its surface Ig-receptor.

 

 

 

Biology of T-Lymphocytes

Thymus-dependent lymphocytes or T-cells are main components of immune system. These cells are immune-specific and function in several regulatory and effector models. The basic prerequisite for their contribution in immune responses is T-cellular antigen recognition. T-lymphocytes are clonally restricted, for each particular clone of them contains unique receptor, able to bind certain antigen.

T lymphocytes arise from stem cells in the bone marrow , that migrate to the thymus, where they undergo differentiation into mature T-cells and then leave the thymus. Mature T cells circulate in the blood and also seed peripheral lymphoid tissues, such as the paracortical areas of lymph nodes and periarteriolar sheaths of the spleen. As mentioned earlier, T and B-lymphocytes are genetically programmed to recognize specific antigens by means of antigen-specific cell-surface receptors. Until recently, the nature of T-cells antigen receptor (TcR) was an enigma. However, recent advances in molecular biology, namely in Southern blot analysis, have provided tools that have allowed the discovery and detailed descriptions of the TcR.

In 95% of T-lymphocytes T-cell receptor (TcR) consists of disulfide-linked heterodimer made up of a - and b - polypeptide chains . They have constant portions, located closely to cell surface, and variable ones, remote from plasma membrane. The variable portions bind the unique sequential antigen determinants.

There is broad polymorphism in the family of TcR, because distal parts of alpha- and beta- chains vary in structure and belong to different clones of T-cells (M. Davies, 1988). The whole molecule of TcR has trans-membrane portion and cytoplasmic tail. The last serves for intracellular transmission of the signal. The tail includes CD3 molecular complex, which itself is composed of at least three invariant proteins. Although antigen recognition is mediated by the variable segments of the TcR, the post-recognition steps involving transfer of activating signals are believed to be the property of CD3 protein complex.

The structure of TcR resembles very closely the architecture of membrane-associated immunoglobulin, utilized by B-cells as an antigen-receptor. Together with HLA molecules, TcR belong to Ig-genes super-family. Recently another population of T-cells was identified, holding gamma- and delta- chains instead of alpha- and beta- ones. These cells have the properties very close to that of common alpha- beta-  T-lymphocytes, but small portion of the 2-nd exon in variable gene of TcR is multiplied. They are not numerous (about 5% of total T-cell amount), but concentrated within RLSAM and RLSAS. The precise role of this minor subtype is not yet completely understood. They may either belong to earlier stages of thymic lymphoid cell maturation, or specialize for integument regional immunity. Unlike TcR alpha- beta- cells, these cells do not express either CD4 or CD8 on the cell surface.

For medical practice, the knowledge that each T cell has a unique DNA rearrangement makes it possible to distinguish polyclonal (non-neoplastic) proliferations of T lymphocytes in lymphoid leukemoid reactions, for example, in viral infections, from monoclonal (neoplastic) proliferations, characteristic for lymphogenous leukemia and lymphomas.

Each and every clone of T-cells has a unique genetically determined structure that is specific for only one of a myriad of antigens.

The mechanisms of their diversity generation are principally analogous to those, responsible for diversity of immunoglobulins (see below). The reshuffling of TcR-encoding genes involves chromosomes 7 and 14. 7th chromosome collects the variants for the genes of variable beta - and gamma - chain peptides; 14th one contains the variants of alpha - and delta - chain peptides. These loci are involved in somatic mutation.

It provides a mechanism by which it possible to have numerous antigen-specific T cells. Because the complete chains of the TcR are produced by a combination of one of the several genes in each of the V, D, J, and C regions – for beta- and gamma- chains; and V, J regions – for alpha- and delta- chains - it is possible to generate enormous diversity in the TcR., resulting from a specific combinatorial rearrangement of different genes within single transcript by means of alternative processing.

The unique TcR complementary binds the complex of a processed antigen and HLA 1st and 2nd class molecules on the surface of APC. This interaction is facilitated and promoted by various additional cell adhesion molecules and leads to the activation of T-cells. Thus, the antigen selectively advances the expansion of the clone, which has specific receptor for it. The cells involved in this complementary interaction secret the autocrine/paracrine signals – cytokines and express cytokine receptors. This process can purposely support high intensity of antigen-stimulated clone expansion.

With the use of monoclonal antibodies it has been possible to use of define several cell-surface markers on developing and mature T cells. These markers are different functional constituents of cell surface, many of them are essential for some crucial steps of T-cell ontogeny. They are briefly characterized in Fig. Along with appropriate markers of B-cells and other elements of immune system. Traditionally they are designated with the abbreviation CD (which means, “ cluster designation” or “cluster of differentiation”) with certain numbers. Some, such as CD3, CD7 and CD2, are present on all peripheral T cells, another few are characteristic for all T and some B-cells (like CD5 or CD28 whereas others define functionally distinct subsets of T-lymphocytes.

In thymus, T-precursors express some T-specific surface antigens, like CD2. They form also accessory molecules, namely CD4 and CD8.

CD4 and CD8 molecules are expressed on two non-overlapping subsets of T cells. Both subsets are represented with the variety of clones, having each distinct TcR molecule able to bind specific complex of the processed antigen and HLA protein. Useless T-cells, not able to bind self HLA-glycoproteins, have perished due to lack of growth stimulation. Autoreactive T-cells, capable to bind the complexes of HLA-proteins and self-antigens presented by thymic dendritic cells, are also extinguished due to hyperstimulation during maturation process. So, when mature T-cells after 3-4 weeks leave thymus for settlement in peripheral lymphoid organs, they are either CD2/CD4-positive or CD2/CD8-positive.

CD4 constitutes the marker on approximately 60% of mature T cells, whereas CD8 is expressed on about 30% of T lymphocytes. Thus, in normal healthy individuals, the CD4 to CD8 ratio is approximately 2:1. This may be altered in various diseases. The AIDS is known for noticeable decrease of this equation due to perish of CD4 elements, because this particular marker can be used by HIV virus as an entrance gates for intracellular penetration.

The T-cell functions, tightly associated with certain CD-markers, may be classified as two broad categories:

1. Cellular immune reactions:

These include several phenomena in which T cells play a pivotal role. Examples are cytotoxic (killer) cells reactive against persistently infected and tumor cells and foreign histocompatibility antigens. Killer T cells are CD3+ (like all mature T cells), but in addition, most of them express CD8. Another expression of cellular immunity is the delayed hypersensibility reaction to be described later. Cytotoxic killer CD8+ T-cells recognize the unique antigens in complex with class I MHC glycoproteins on any cells and are able to kill their target cells directly, by means of perforin-mediated lysis.

 

2. Regulatory functions:T cells also regulate the function and growth of other T-cells, B cells, hematopoetic and, probably, also other somatic cells. Their immunoregulating functions are expressed either as facilitation or as suppression of the response. Thus some CD4+ helper-inducer cells provide "help" in the generation of cytotoxic T cells and antibody-secreting B cells. CD4 molecules, characteristic for major part of this subset, bind to class II molecules of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC or HLA). T-helpers control many cellular functions including proliferation, differentiation and isotypic switching of B-cells by means of different cytokines. If TcR receptor of a T-helper corresponds to the complex of HLA-II molecule and processed antigen on the B–lymphocyte, this starts the activation of T-helper with the release of interleukins. T-helpers may belong to Th1 either Th2 subtypes. The differentiation of Th1 is promoted by interleukin-12, for Th2 autocrine growth and differentiation promoter is interleukin-13. Being activated, Th1 secret preferentially interleukin-2 and g - interferon. At the same time, Th2 besides these regulators (and in predominant quantity) produce large amount of interleukin-4 (switching factor for B-lymphocytes immunoglobulin synthesis swap for IgG1 and IgE), of interleukin-5 (switching plasma cells for IgA biosynthesis and promoting proliferation and activation of eosinophils) and of interleukin-10 (blocker of Th1 and g - interferon production). Th2 produce growing quantities of interleukin-13, which shift the balance of immunoglobulins towards production of reagins. So, the interaction between different CD4-lymphocytes and sequential antigenic determinants play an important role in determination of predominant antibody class and generation of different immediate hypersensitivity reactions.

T-cell help (through production of interleukin-2) is also of great significance in cytotoxic component of delayed hypersensitivity.

Prevention and arrest of immune response traditionally is characterized as specific lymphocyte function called suppression.

Some subsets of CD8+ cells, CD4+ cells, as well as certain B-cells serve to suppress the immune responses. These regulatory functions are mediated for the most part by the secretion of soluble cytokines, but also through incomplete killing effect and even via anti-idiotypic autoantibodies. Both direct, antigen specific and non-direct antigen-specific suppression play an important part in regulation of immune response, in establishment of peripheral tolerance and control of autoimmunity (Fig. ). Classic concept of suppression mechanisms (M. Feldman, 1972; N.K. Damle, 1986) use to operate with the idea of special subset of T-suppressor lymphocytes, responsible exclusively for different (non-specific, idiotype-specific and antigen-specific) kinds of suppressing effect. Current concept denies the existence of lymphoid subset, specialized exclusively on suppression (A. Cook, 1994). In truth, suppressing functions are distributed between:

  • Th2 , which secret immunosuppressive cytokines;
  • CD8-positive cytotoxic lymphocytes, using incomplete killing effect for direct clonal suppression;
  • B-cells elaborating antiidiotypic autoantibodies (indirect idiotype-specific suppression). It has been demonstrated that small doses of antiidiotypes are able to trigger antigen-specific immune response, activating memory cells in the absence of an antigen itself. At the same time, their large doses suppress the antigen-specific immune response (K. Rajevsky, A. Takemori; 1983, A. Kulberg, 1986). CD8-positive cells are able to render suppressor action by means of free TcR secretion.

All the facts mentioned above witness that suppression as a phenomenon really exists, but suppressors as the separate from killers, helpers and B-cells subset of T-cells are mythical!

In response to specific antigen complementary to TcR or after non-specific boosting with some mitogens, T-cells are triggered into the process of activation. The peripheral T-cells are mostly long-living ones and stay in G0 or G1 phases of the cell cycle (Fig. , external greater circle). Upon activation by certain signals, they can bear one of the following functions: proliferation, differentiation, and cytokine production.

The complex of an antigen and HLA-protein, correspondent to the clonal TcR, achieves specific monoclonal activation of T-cells (as well as B-cells). This results in blast-transformation of T-lymphocytes with subsequent chain of cell divisions (8-10 times). In blast-transformation, G0 and G1 phases get exceedingly shortened S-periods and mitoses follow one after another (Fig. ; Internal smaller circle).

Non-specific polyclonal activation of T- (and also B-) lymphocytes is started by polyclonal activators or mitogens, such as plant lectins, concanavallin A or phytohemagglutinin. These agents bind the surface mitogenic receptors of lymphocytes, located on many various clones, so the response involves simultaneously a lot of different lymphoid clones. As a rule, polyclonal response is much weaker in comparison with regular monoclonal one, provoked by antigen-specific activation. Mitogenic stimulation, in difference with antigenic blast-transformation, does not trigger the serial cell divisions, but is limited with one mitosis.

Recently, a new class of T-cell activation messengers was discovered: so called “super-antigens”. Commonly, these are microbial products, but some foodstuff and prescribed drugs also belong to this group. The super-antigens are able to stimulate the T-cells carrying some variant of TcR in oligoclonal manner (Fig. ). Super-antigenicity is relative property: the same substance may be super-antigenic for one individual and indifferent for another. The mandatory attribute of a super-antigen is simultaneous correspondence between a portion of its molecule – and some unique variant of TcR, and between another portion of the same super-antigen molecule – and HLA class II glycoprotein (G.M. Johnson et al., 1992).

Super-antigens regardless of their TcR antigenic specificity, which is bypassed, activate cD4-positive lymphocytes. Activation of multiple T-clones by super-antigens results in massive production of various cytokines – interleukin-2, 6, 8; TNF, etc. These cytokines in greater doses display some systemic actions including fever, vomiting, diarrhea and even toxic-septic shock.

Sometimes, super-antigens, presented by B-lymphocytes activate T-helpers. In this situation there is parallel stimulation of B-lymphocytes themselves. Quite typically it is manifested in activation of autoreactive B-clones and precipitation of autoimmune syndromes.

The diversity of super-antigens includes gram-negative bacteria lipopolysaccharides, staphylococcus E enterotoxin, toxic shock syndrome toxin, Epstein - Barr virus and even components of kiwi fruit.

Super-antigens are of considerable importance for Pathophysiology of many allergoid reactions, including toxic dyspepsia in infants and food toxicoinfection in adults.

Biology Of The B Lymphocytes And Plasma Cells

Maturing of B-cells takes place both before and after birth. The bone marrow precursors of primitive B-cells express cytoplasmic m - chains of immunoglobulins, but their surface is free from antibody molecules. More differentiated, but still immature precursors of B-lymphocytes display both intact cytoplasmic and surface IgM. Mature B-cells have their cytoplasmic IgM lost, but still keep the surface one. In addition, they express surface IgD.

These changes develop under the influence of cytokines and are referred to as antigen-independent differentiation. Interestingly, the major part of functionally active antibody-secreting plasma cells is devoid of any surface immunoglobulins, but carry surface marker PC-1, up to date (1998) still not included into CD-system.

The B cell surface immunoglobulin can be identified by fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry after the cells have been incubated with anti-immunoglobulin antibodies labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate. This procedure is utilized clinically to identify and quantitate B cells. In addition, as with T cells, several monoclonal antibodies directed against B cell-surface molecules have been described. CD20 is ionic channel, specific distinctive marker of all B-cells. All B-cells also express globotriosyl-ceramide known as CD77. Some of the B cell-specific surface markers, such as CD19 (formerly termed B4), appear early in B-cell differentiation, prior to expression of surface immunoglobulin, and are therefore useful in determining the histogenesis of certain leukemias of childhood. It is essential for evaluation of clinical prognosis in lymphoblastic leukemia, because the forms originated from different stages of B-cell development have strikingly different percentage of remissions induced by the same treatment. For example, leukemia clones derived from early pre-B cells are more prone to treatment that those originated from later stages.

  • Mature B lymphocytes are present in the blood and lymphoid tissue, including bone marrow. In human blood they constitute 10 to 20% of the lymphocyte population. In lymph nodes they are found in the superficial cortex, forming lymphoid follicles. In the spleen they live in the white pulp organized as lymphoid follicles, usually having pale-staining central areas called germinal centers.

The genetic basis for the clonal diversity of the B-cell repertoire (i.e., the existence of numerous B cells programmed to recognize only one antigen) has been clarified by the studies that were initiated by S. Tonegava. There are remarkable similarities in the organization of immunoglobulin genes and genes of the T-cell receptor, discussed below (see “Immunoglobulins”).

Non-active mature B-cells can be stimulated for growth, hypertrophy of synthetic apparatus, division and secretion of antibodies. The triggers for this sequence of events may be different: specific (antigens) and non-specific (mitogens and super-antigens). In last case the mode of activation is polyclonal. As a rule, antigenic stimulation of B-lymphocytes requires not only an antigen with conformation determinant, correspondent to surface immunoglobulin receptor. The effective production of antibodies is achieved with participation of T-helpers. Antigen-specific T-helper clone expands after presentation of sequential antigen determinants by APC to T-cells (see above). Activated T-helpers secrete the cytokines (e.g. interleukin-2), which, along with specific antigen, precisely stimulate the blast-transformation and subsequent expansion of particular specific B-clone. This clone is transfigured into plasma cells, highly efficient factories of circulating antibodies.

In the course of immune response there occurs a swap of Ig-classes (isotypic switching). Initially, plasma cells produce IgM, later in primary response (or from the very beginning of boosted immune response) different isotypes other than IgM are prevailing. These may be IgG, IgA or IgE of the same paratopic specificity

It has been clearly shown that interleukin-4 and interleukin-13, produced by T-cells, are responsible for IgE synthesis, interleukin-5 promotes, at least along with other stimulants, the secretion of IgA; and g - interferon, as well as interleukin-12, decrease the generation of IgE. Some antigens contain multiple repeats of identical epitopes. Due to this, they are able to cross-link the surface immunoglobulin receptors of B-cells. As a result of such unusual mode of stimulation, B-lymphocytes may get activation without contribution of T-cells. This group of antigens is referred to as T-independent”. In fact, these are analogues of the mitogens, acting via mechanism very close to that described in previous section. The sense of their action over B-lymphocytes is, probably, explained by the evolutionary necessity to establish the response for bacterial challenge as early as possible. Among “T-independent” antigens are dextran, ficoll, some other bacterial polysaccharides, few proteins of Flagellata etc. Commonly, immune system rapidly responds for them with IgM and the strength of the reaction is not so great. Polyclonal stimulation of B-cells can be achieved also by Epstein-Barr virus, bound to CD21 marker of mature B-cells and follicular dendritic cells. This kind of polyclonal stimulation is responsible for the symptoms of infectious mononucleosis. Intensive production of cytokines causes high fever and activation of autoreactive B-clones leads to multi-systemic involvement in this disease and positive Paul-Bunnell test. The stimulated lymphocytes are enlarged, with big nuclei and considerable amount of basophilic cytoplasm. This unusual appearance was a reason to call them “virocytes” or in classic texts “lymphomonocytes” (I.A. Kassirsky), although they are purely lymphoid in their origin.

From the cellular level of discussion about the constituents of immune system, we can now dive deeper – to the molecular contributors of immunologic processes.

Immunoglobulins as markers and recognizing tools

The immunoglobulins (Ig) or antibodies, which is the same, are products of functionally active B-cells (plasma cells). (Fig ) They consist of polypeptide chains with varying configuration. There are 5 main classes, or isotypes of Ig; also subclasses are distinguished. Paul Ehrlich (1901) prophetically postulated the existence of antibodies as “circulating receptors separated from the surface of cell”. Soon it was discovered, that Ig belong to globulin fraction of blood serum and mostly migrate with its g - fraction (A. Tiselius, 1930-1948). R.R. Porter and J.M. Edelman (1957-1962) in general, decrypted the structure of Ig.

Ig molecules consist of polypeptides, joined together with disulfide bridges. They have characteristic spatial conformation and contain some carbohydrates, although non-protein moieties are not related to specific part of antibodies.

The main carcass of antibody consists of heavy (H) and light (L) chains. The example is IgG molecule, having two H- and two L- identical chains. Different portions of the whole Ig molecule serve for various functions. The portions with NH2-ends of heavy and light chains (Fab –Fragments antigen-binding) are variable, cause they are different in primary structure in antibodies of various specificity. Variable regions form a concave of unique shape, filled up with appropriate antigenic epitope. This end of molecule is used for binding of an antigen and bears the immunologic specificity of an antibody. Epitope-fixing area is peculiar in every antibody of a given specificity, and is called paratope. Paratope is, in turn, antigenic. The whole paratope, or its part, involved in immunologic interactions as an antigen is called idiotоpe, and the set of idiotopes located on specific antibody is known as idiotype. The variants of heavy chains antigenic structure, determined by allelic polymorphism of appropriate genes, which are characteristic for somatotype of certain groups of individuals, are called allotypes.

Hydroxyl COOH-end of Ig molecule is called Fc-portion (Fragment crystallizable or constant). It has the same structure in all antibodies of every particular class and subclass, but differs in Ig, belonging to different subclasses and classes (isotypic variance). Fc-portion fulfills different biological functions in various classes of Ig, making them tools for different purposes.

For instance, unique ability of IgG trespass the placental barrier depends on architecture of its Fc-portion. Fc-portion of Ig embodies the loci for binding of some important ligands: complement proteins, staphylococcal A and streptococcal G-proteins, receptors of macrophages and NK-cells (which is mandatory in phagocytosis and antibody-mediated cytotoxicity). So, this is a portion, essential for realization of antibody effector functions. Pepsin lyses Ig with liberation of (Fab)’2 и Fc-portions, and papain action gives two separate mono-Fab fragments and Fc-fragment together with mixture of short peptides – products of hinge region hydrolysis (R. Pumphrey). Every light and heavy chain is polypeptide, composed by means of transcription and translation of genes, encoding constant and variable portions of Ig. This is achieved through complex process of gene splicing (S. Tonegava, 1987-1991).

Antibody diversity is based on the fact that every individual B-lymphocyte and all its clonal descendents produce an immunoglobulin with unique spatial structure of paratope. Idiotypes never repeat. Variable portions of light and heavy chains are encoded by V-genes. This diversity could not be possible, if each particular Ig was encoded entirely by single unique large gene, from the very beginning till the end of its primary structure (like, for example, interleukins really are encoded).

S. Tonegava have demonstrated that heterogeneity of antibodies is achieved because different parts of every polypeptide in their molecules are encoded by different germ line genes, and each of this genes exists in genome as multi-gene, in numerous variants. Thus, in mice the immunoglobulin heavy chain is assembled from a pool of approximately 200 V, 12 D, and 4 J segments. Moreover, these multi-genes are objects of extremely frequent somatic mutation during ontogenesis of B-cells, which increases the degree of variance. The transposons – mobile dispersed portions of genome able to change their position in chromosomes during ontogenesis – are of greatest significance for Ig diversity generation (B. McClintock, 1983). The shadowed side of this effective “program files reshuffling” mechanism is relatively high risk of translocations, causing protooncogene expression in lymphoid cells This makes lymphomas and lymphogenous leukemias quite frequent among malignancies (6th place in incidence for North American population, according the statistics of 1997). . During B cell maturation there is somatic rearrangement of the DNA, as was described for T cells. Given the numbers of genes in the V,, D, and J regions for heavy chains and light chains, it is not difficult to estimate that antibodies with greater that 10 specificites can arise by unique combinatorial rearrangements of the immunoglobulin genes. As mentioned earlier with respect to T cells, it is important to note the practical application of this knowledge. Because in a given B cell and its progeny only one of the several million possible DNA rearrangements occurs, it is feasible to distinguish between monoclonal (neoplastic) and polyclonal (reactive or leukemoid) proliferations of B-cells by extracting the DNA and subjecting it to Southern blot analysis.

So, the structure of antibody molecule varies due to shuffle and splicing of m-RNA, obtained by means of alternating transcription of above-mentioned gene variants. There is persuasive hypothesis, that Ig diversity is a result of evolutionary divergence of few genes-precursors, initially, probably, encoded the enzymes glycosyltransferases (S. Roth; 1985). Besides alternating transcription of multi-gene portions and their somatic mutation, additional source of diversity is a drift of fusion point for polypeptides spliced (sutured) into single Ig molecule. The variants for L-chains are kept in chromosome 2 and 22, for H-chain – in chromosome 14.

For didactic purposes all these sources of antibodies diversity can be resumed as follows:

  • There are many alternative variants of germ line genes for variable areas of Ig L- and H-chains
  • Assembly of entire H-chain includes free recombination of products, encoded by one of many V, one of many J, and one of many D-regions, assembly of L-chain goes almost identically, but with one of numerous V and one of several J-region products, because the immunoglobulin light chain genes have a similar organization, except that they lack the D-region.
  • In recombination, fusion point drifts and is not precisely located, thus changing the structure of Ig.
  • V-genes of Ig are most mutable among all somatic genes, so 9 out of every 10 B-clones carry some mutant Ig-genes, absent in germ line native set.
  • Any H-chain can be combined with any L-chain.
  • In addition, there are eight constant region genes (Cm , Cg , Ca , and so on) corresponding to the immunoglobulin isotypes and their subclasses.

The same clone of B-cells can switch Ig biosynthesis from one isotype to another, not altering Ig antigenic specificity. This is achieved by poly-adenylation of primary transcript, containing RNA-copies of different C-genes in alternative points. Owing to this variation, transcript may loss in splicing different C-portions. This is regulated by cytokines and complement fragments and proceeds differently in non-boosted and boosted immune responses.

As a result of this complicated process, in ready Ig L-chains and H-chains both contain variable and constant areas, last ones are located in NH2-ends. There are light chains of 2 classes: k - and l -. Heavy chains are of 5 classes -g , a , m , d and e . These specifics correspond to 5 classes of Ig – G, A, M, D and E.

IgG – is a main monomer class of circulating Ig, migrating electrophoretically within its g -fraction. It is most active antiviral and antibacterial factor and potent opsonin, leading contributor of boosted immune response and single class of Ig, able to neutralize toxins. IgG is able yo penetrate across placental barrier and establish passive immunity in newborn for the period up to 3-6 months. Its half-life time in serum is 3 weeks. IgG, along with IgM activates classic pathway of complement system. There are 4 subclasses of IgG: IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4. They differ in small details of H-chain structure in Fc-portion. Antibodies of IgG2-subclass mostly function as opsonins and respond for toxins and dextrans. IgG1 and IgG2 in humans frequently account for anti-rhesus reactions. IgG4 in humans display significant affinity to mast cells and basophils, taking part in anaphylaxis, but IgG1 are completely devoid of anaphylactic potency. In rodents, for example, in guinea pig, the situation with IgG subclasses and anaphylaxis is exactly inverted.
IgA – in humans is presented in sera with monomer (80%) and dimer (20%). In other mammals dimeric form is predominant. IgA is specifically related to the functions of regional intro-barrier lymphoid systems associated with integument. It is main secretory product of lymphoid elements in respiratory, urogenital and gut mucosa. IgA also establish humoral immune defense in breast milk, colostrum, sweat and sebum, produced by skin glands. Secretory IgA, especially that of gut, has peculiar configuration, resistant to proteolysis. This form is dimeric, with special connective polypeptide chain, called secretory fragment. It is suggested that IgA is most effective against germ invasion via mucosa membranes. There are 2 subclasses of this antibody: IgA1 and IgA2. Electrophoretically it migrates not only with g - but also with b -globulin fractions. Immune complexes with IgA have quite slow clearance, which sometimes make them the contributors of immune complex disorders.
IgM or macroglobulin is a large molecule including 5 subunits, each comprised of  2 m - chains and 2 light chains. The subuhits form the vagon-wheel structure with Fc-portions looking inwards, bound together by connective chains. IgM presents not only in serum, but also in the cytoplasm and on the plasma membrane of immature B-cells. This is first class of antibodies, starting primary immune response. About 5 % of B-cells, those marked by CD5, produce spontaneously and after polyclonal activation autoreactive IgM of low affinity, directed towards non-tissue specific epitopes, like DNA, heat shock proteins and cytoskeleton elements- so called natural autoantibodies. Their excessive activity is blamed for some cases of autoimmune disorders. Unlike IgG, which is mostly serum Ig, macroglobulin distribution is balanced between blood and tissues. IgM is potent activator of complement classic pathway, highly efficient opsonin and agglutinating agent. Cold hemagglutinins, heterophilic antibodies and isohemagglutinins – all belong to this particular class of Ig. T-independent antigens trigger, preferentially, IgM-response. In controversy with traditional interpretation of Igs as g - globulin, IgM migrates in electrophoresis of blood serum with b - globulins and, partially, even with a 2 - globulins. Immune complexes with IgM in healthy person have relatively effective clearance.
IgD has 2 d -chains and two light ones. It is characteristic for the surface of immature B-cells and present in serum in trace low concentrations, although last is increased in polyclonal B-immunostimulation and paradoxally, in some innate immune deficits. The real significance of IgD is not yet completely explained. M. Owen and M. Stewart believe that IgD takes part in antigen-dependent differentiation of B-cells (1994).
IgE –  reagins or homocytotropic antibodies. Their classic function is to provoke hyperergic immunity and inflammation in response to macroparasites. The greater is their production in mammals and humans, the stronger is anti-helminthes resistance. They also play some physiological roles in placenta formation. IgE is blamed for atopic disorders and anaphylaxis. The major part of their unique properties is dictated by the presence of specific domain in Fc-portion. This domain is able to bind Fce R-I receptors of mast cells and basophils with high affinity. IgE-receptors of lower affinity (Fce R-2) may be expressed of the surface of other cells, later in course of anaphylactic reactions. Virtually all WBC, platelets, alveolar macrophages and (in patients with atopic dermatitis) also skin APC – Langerhans’ cells are able to express IgE-sensitive receptors under the influence of IL-4. When Fab-fragments of these receptor-bound IgE cross-link with a molecule of antigen, mast cell or another reagin-sensitized element starts degranulation and release of inflammation mediators, causing hyperergic anaphylactic reactions.

Small concentrations of IgE are present in tissues and blood plasma. The ability to produce small amount of IgE is controlled by dominant gene. Several different genes, controlling interleukin spectrum, interferon reception, T-helper behavior as well as genes of HLA, are associated with abnormally high IgE production. Increased synthesis of IgE is multifactorial sign, and its additive expression is influenced by many environmental factors. Individuals, predisposed to excessively high IgE production are so called hyperreactors. Their constitutional feature may manifest in wide variety of atopic diseases (hay fever, vasomotor rhinitis, bronchial asthma, Quinque’s oedema, atopic dermatitis and many others described in section “Allergy”).

Mucosal Immunity

The Regional Lymphoid Systems Associated with the Mucosa (RLSAM) are presented with multiple non-encapsulated lymphoid elements, residing in lamina propria of the mucous membranes and in submucosal layer. RLSAM are covered with basal membranes and superficial epithelia, which contain mucous and other glands and secretory goblet cells. The crossing point of gut and respiratory tract is a place of extensive and broad antigenic stimulation. Non-occasionally, it is the location of Waldeier-Pirogov ring, which consists of such RLSAM elements: tonsils. These are often involved in inflammation in sore throat (tonsillitis). There are palatial, nasopharyngeal, laryngeal, and tongue tonsils. The trachea, ENT sinuses and bronchi also have RLSAM elements. RLSAM of respiratory mucosa includes also lymphoid elements of terminal respiratory tracts and chylus lymph nodes. Respiratory RLSAM is responsible for synthesis and secretion of Immunoglobulin A (IgA) during immune reactions provoked by inhaled antigens. Local lymphoid elements exist within lamina propria and submucosa of urogenital tracts as well. Gut associated lymphoid tissue is quite abundant and includes intestinal units – Peyer’s patches and lymphoid elements of the appendix. Polyclonal immunostimulation of appendix lymphoid cells with certain antigens (for example, that of Yersenia) may cause the clinical picture of acute appendicitis. The hepatic lymphoid cells cooperate with local macrophages (totally representing in rodents about 30 % of liver mass) and also belong to RLSAM.

These gut elements respond for ingested antigens and also are able to generate IgA.

Totally RLSAM is presented mostly with cytotoxic and helper T-cells (up to 70%). There are also less than 30% B-cells forming germinal follicles, mostly within lamina propria. Plasma cells of these compartments produce IgA. Submucosal layer contains almost exclusively T-cells. The specific of intraepithelial RLSAM T-cells is peculiar type of surface T-cell receptor. They belong to minor subset of T-lymphocytes with g s - type of antigenic receptors. It is believed that their specialization is related to immune surveillance over mutant cells and cells containing persistent intracellular pathogens.

Unlike other classes of antibodies, IgA are subdued to endocytosis into the cells of mucosa epithelium via special polyglobulin receptor. Within these non-lymphoid cells IgA join to special secretory component (of epithelial origin), which protects them from proteolysis during the process of transcytosis. Finally, IgA are excreted with mucus, bile or other secrets on the surface of mucosa or into the lumen of an organ (K. Mostoff, R. Blobel; 1982).

RLSAM is sequestered from other peripheral lymphoid organs due to its “intro-barrier” (A.S. Shevelyov, 1992) location. During immune responses it is able to react as a whole entity. Cause of this, local sensitization of the lymphocytes in one of RLSAM compartments some time later may be transfigured into systemic hypersensitivity and boosted immune response occurs through all its surface. Moreover, cross-reactions may occur with enteric rout of sensitization and boosted immune response over respiratory tract mucosa – for example in food allergy associated with bronchial asthma. These phenomena exist owing to unique routs of lymphocyte circulation and homing, characteristic for mucosa associated lymphoid tissue. The intestinal mucosa contains special antigen-transporting M-cells, which absorb the gut antigens, process them and fetch to lymphocytes. After contact with a gut antigen, many of local lymphocytes penetrate into systemic bloodstream and, later, return back home via intestinal HEVs. This is established by means of recognition of unique addressin molecules, expressed exclusively at the place of gut lymphocyte destination. Gut-associated lymphocytes are very essential for the development of tolerance towards enteric antigens. The lytic abilities of digestive system protect intestinal lymphocytes from excessive antigenic challenge. There is some kind of local balance on the mucosa membranes of gut, achieved between normal microflora and lymphocytes. This system stays in the state of symbiosis and does not display too strong immune responses against normal inhabitants of healthy man’s gut (status known as eubacteriosis). The breast-feeding facilitates the formation of such immune-bacterial balance. Under some pathologic conditions, digestion and decay of certain antigens and/or polyclonal lymphoid stimulators is not efficient or incomplete. Due to imbalance of antigenic environment, gut lymphocytes may respond with hypersensitivity or even autoallergic reactions.

A bright example is one of the mechanisms in juvenile sugar diabetes (insulin dependent Diabetes Mellitus – IDDM) pathogenesis.

The enteric immunization with some peptides derived from bovine serum (milk) albumin in artificial feeding of infants, especially, premature babies is risky for subsequent provocation of autoallergy against self-insulin. This is typical for the carriers of D3 and D4 HLA antigens, who poorly distinguish between self and alien peptides, and may even cause progressive autoimmune insulitis, b - cell insufficiency and IDDM onset (A. Karjalainen et al.; 1992). Non-digested cereal proteins - avenin and gliadin may be retained within gut mucosa and act as polyclonal lectin-like immunostimulants. They boost different local lymphocytes, including autoreactive ones. This mechanism is observed in chronic autoimmune atrophic enteritis of celiakia (sprue) patients. Cross-reaction of intestinal RLSAM between the antigens of non-pathogenic E. Coli and bowel mucosa may contribute into mechanisms of non-specific ulcerative colitis etc.

Regional Lymphoid System Associated With Skin

Similarly to RLSAM, it is possible to delineate regional lymphoid system associated with skin (RLSAS). The antigens, penetrating via skin, are engulfed and presented by epidermal Langerhans cells – a kind of skin APC. Langerhans cells are able to cooperate with the lymphocytes directly within the skin or after migration – into regional lymph nodes.

This compartment of immune system is of greatest importance for the development of contact dermatitis, tuberculin-like reactions, bullous pemphigus, psoriasis and many others immunopathological skin disease. Skin plasma cells are also able to produce IgA, which penetrates here into the discharge of sweat and sebaceous skin glands.

 

 

Blood

The blood itself is an constitutive part of the immune system. All lymphoid and non-lymphoid cell lines as well as their humoral products are presented within bloodstream .

Blood samples are commonly taken for clinical evaluation of immune system parameters (WBC total number and differential count, lymphoid subsets percentage, immunoglobulins level, circulating immune complexes concentration, cytokines content etc.). Sometimes the picture revealed in peripheral blood smear corresponds to the data, characteristic for other lymphoid organs. For example, in AIDS the same shifts in CD4 and CD8-positive lymphocytes equation are emblematic both for peripheral blood and bone marrow.

In sarcoidosis involved lung tissue and peripheral blood have different subset content of lymphocytes. In demyelinizing disorders of CNS (multiple sclerosis, lateral amyotrophic sclerosis, Guillen-Barr syndrome) blood lymphocytes and intrathecal lymphoid cells of cerebrospinal fluid are principally different in their activity and set of surface markers. The blood is an arena for “graft versus host” reactions. In mismatched blood transfusion it is the scene of “host versus graft” cytotoxic response.

Now we shall discuss the "cellular composition " of the immune response.

Antigen presenting cells (APC)

The system of mononuclear phagocytes and some closely related kinds of cells (glial, endothelial, and thymic stellate cells of ectodermal origin) basically, perform the function of the antigen-presentation.

.  

Macrophages are a part of the mononuclear phagocyte system, descendants of bone marrow promonocytes and peripheral blood monocytes. Macrophages leave bloodstream and emigrate in different tissues, where acquire some specific features and are called by different names (histiocytes, osteoclasts, alveolar, peritoneal, pleural macrophages, Kupffer’s cells etc.). However, having the same origin and occupation they all belong to the same dynasty of mononuclear phagocytes and play an important role in inflammation. Besides macrophages realize essential functions in the immune response.

A branch of this dynasty is represented by dendritic and Langerhans' cells.

This group includes a population of cells that are all characterized by dendritic cytoplasmic processes and the presence of large amounts of cell-surface class II HLA molecules. Dendritic cells are found in the lymphoid tissues, and somewhat similar cells within the epidermis have been called Langerhans' cells. By virtue of cell-surface class II molecules, dendritic cells and Langerhans' cells are extremely efficient in antigen presentation, and some investigators believe that they are the most important antigen-presenting cells in the body. It should be noted that although they share antigen-presenting capacity with macrophages, unlike the latter cell type they are weakly phagocytic.

The main antigen-processing cell for B-lymphocytes is dendritic reticular cell of lymphoid follicles. For T-lymphocytes it is interdigiting reticular cell of lymph node paracortical zone.

 

APC clones are not restricted with processing of certain unique antigen, but rather able to present any heteroantigens or autoantigens to lymphoid cells of appropriate clonal specificity. B cell themselves also may present antigens in association with MHC-proteins, but, unlike APC, it is clonally restricted with one antigen – that, recognized by its surface Ig-receptor.

 

 

 

Biology of T-Lymphocytes

Thymus-dependent lymphocytes or T-cells are main components of immune system. These cells are immune-specific and function in several regulatory and effector models. The basic prerequisite for their contribution in immune responses is T-cellular antigen recognition. T-lymphocytes are clonally restricted, for each particular clone of them contains unique receptor, able to bind certain antigen.

T lymphocytes arise from stem cells in the bone marrow , that migrate to the thymus, where they undergo differentiation into mature T-cells and then leave the thymus. Mature T cells circulate in the blood and also seed peripheral lymphoid tissues, such as the paracortical areas of lymph nodes and periarteriolar sheaths of the spleen. As mentioned earlier, T and B-lymphocytes are genetically programmed to recognize specific antigens by means of antigen-specific cell-surface receptors. Until recently, the nature of T-cells antigen receptor (TcR) was an enigma. However, recent advances in molecular biology, namely in Southern blot analysis, have provided tools that have allowed the discovery and detailed descriptions of the TcR.

In 95% of T-lymphocytes T-cell receptor (TcR) consists of disulfide-linked heterodimer made up of a - and b - polypeptide chains . They have constant portions, located closely to cell surface, and variable ones, remote from plasma membrane. The variable portions bind the unique sequential antigen determinants.

There is broad polymorphism in the family of TcR, because distal parts of alpha- and beta- chains vary in structure and belong to different clones of T-cells (M. Davies, 1988). The whole molecule of TcR has trans-membrane portion and cytoplasmic tail. The last serves for intracellular transmission of the signal. The tail includes CD3 molecular complex, which itself is composed of at least three invariant proteins. Although antigen recognition is mediated by the variable segments of the TcR, the post-recognition steps involving transfer of activating signals are believed to be the property of CD3 protein complex.

The structure of TcR resembles very closely the architecture of membrane-associated immunoglobulin, utilized by B-cells as an antigen-receptor. Together with HLA molecules, TcR belong to Ig-genes super-family. Recently another population of T-cells was identified, holding gamma- and delta- chains instead of alpha- and beta- ones. These cells have the properties very close to that of common alpha- beta-  T-lymphocytes, but small portion of the 2-nd exon in variable gene of TcR is multiplied. They are not numerous (about 5% of total T-cell amount), but concentrated within RLSAM and RLSAS. The precise role of this minor subtype is not yet completely understood. They may either belong to earlier stages of thymic lymphoid cell maturation, or specialize for integument regional immunity. Unlike TcR alpha- beta- cells, these cells do not express either CD4 or CD8 on the cell surface.

For medical practice, the knowledge that each T cell has a unique DNA rearrangement makes it possible to distinguish polyclonal (non-neoplastic) proliferations of T lymphocytes in lymphoid leukemoid reactions, for example, in viral infections, from monoclonal (neoplastic) proliferations, characteristic for lymphogenous leukemia and lymphomas.

Each and every clone of T-cells has a unique genetically determined structure that is specific for only one of a myriad of antigens.

The mechanisms of their diversity generation are principally analogous to those, responsible for diversity of immunoglobulins (see below). The reshuffling of TcR-encoding genes involves chromosomes 7 and 14. 7th chromosome collects the variants for the genes of variable beta - and gamma - chain peptides; 14th one contains the variants of alpha - and delta - chain peptides. These loci are involved in somatic mutation.

It provides a mechanism by which it possible to have numerous antigen-specific T cells. Because the complete chains of the TcR are produced by a combination of one of the several genes in each of the V, D, J, and C regions – for beta- and gamma- chains; and V, J regions – for alpha- and delta- chains - it is possible to generate enormous diversity in the TcR., resulting from a specific combinatorial rearrangement of different genes within single transcript by means of alternative processing.

The unique TcR complementary binds the complex of a processed antigen and HLA 1st and 2nd class molecules on the surface of APC. This interaction is facilitated and promoted by various additional cell adhesion molecules and leads to the activation of T-cells. Thus, the antigen selectively advances the expansion of the clone, which has specific receptor for it. The cells involved in this complementary interaction secret the autocrine/paracrine signals – cytokines and express cytokine receptors. This process can purposely support high intensity of antigen-stimulated clone expansion.

With the use of monoclonal antibodies it has been possible to use of define several cell-surface markers on developing and mature T cells. These markers are different functional constituents of cell surface, many of them are essential for some crucial steps of T-cell ontogeny. They are briefly characterized in Fig. Along with appropriate markers of B-cells and other elements of immune system. Traditionally they are designated with the abbreviation CD (which means, “ cluster designation” or “cluster of differentiation”) with certain numbers. Some, such as CD3, CD7 and CD2, are present on all peripheral T cells, another few are characteristic for all T and some B-cells (like CD5 or CD28 whereas others define functionally distinct subsets of T-lymphocytes.

In thymus, T-precursors express some T-specific surface antigens, like CD2. They form also accessory molecules, namely CD4 and CD8.

CD4 and CD8 molecules are expressed on two non-overlapping subsets of T cells. Both subsets are represented with the variety of clones, having each distinct TcR molecule able to bind specific complex of the processed antigen and HLA protein. Useless T-cells, not able to bind self HLA-glycoproteins, have perished due to lack of growth stimulation. Autoreactive T-cells, capable to bind the complexes of HLA-proteins and self-antigens presented by thymic dendritic cells, are also extinguished due to hyperstimulation during maturation process. So, when mature T-cells after 3-4 weeks leave thymus for settlement in peripheral lymphoid organs, they are either CD2/CD4-positive or CD2/CD8-positive.

CD4 constitutes the marker on approximately 60% of mature T cells, whereas CD8 is expressed on about 30% of T lymphocytes. Thus, in normal healthy individuals, the CD4 to CD8 ratio is approximately 2:1. This may be altered in various diseases. The AIDS is known for noticeable decrease of this equation due to perish of CD4 elements, because this particular marker can be used by HIV virus as an entrance gates for intracellular penetration.

The T-cell functions, tightly associated with certain CD-markers, may be classified as two broad categories:

1. Cellular immune reactions:

These include several phenomena in which T cells play a pivotal role. Examples are cytotoxic (killer) cells reactive against persistently infected and tumor cells and foreign histocompatibility antigens. Killer T cells are CD3+ (like all mature T cells), but in addition, most of them express CD8. Another expression of cellular immunity is the delayed hypersensibility reaction to be described later. Cytotoxic killer CD8+ T-cells recognize the unique antigens in complex with class I MHC glycoproteins on any cells and are able to kill their target cells directly, by means of perforin-mediated lysis.

 

2. Regulatory functions:T cells also regulate the function and growth of other T-cells, B cells, hematopoetic and, probably, also other somatic cells. Their immunoregulating functions are expressed either as facilitation or as suppression of the response. Thus some CD4+ helper-inducer cells provide "help" in the generation of cytotoxic T cells and antibody-secreting B cells. CD4 molecules, characteristic for major part of this subset, bind to class II molecules of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC or HLA). T-helpers control many cellular functions including proliferation, differentiation and isotypic switching of B-cells by means of different cytokines. If TcR receptor of a T-helper corresponds to the complex of HLA-II molecule and processed antigen on the B–lymphocyte, this starts the activation of T-helper with the release of interleukins. T-helpers may belong to Th1 either Th2 subtypes. The differentiation of Th1 is promoted by interleukin-12, for Th2 autocrine growth and differentiation promoter is interleukin-13. Being activated, Th1 secret preferentially interleukin-2 and g - interferon. At the same time, Th2 besides these regulators (and in predominant quantity) produce large amount of interleukin-4 (switching factor for B-lymphocytes immunoglobulin synthesis swap for IgG1 and IgE), of interleukin-5 (switching plasma cells for IgA biosynthesis and promoting proliferation and activation of eosinophils) and of interleukin-10 (blocker of Th1 and g - interferon production). Th2 produce growing quantities of interleukin-13, which shift the balance of immunoglobulins towards production of reagins. So, the interaction between different CD4-lymphocytes and sequential antigenic determinants play an important role in determination of predominant antibody class and generation of different immediate hypersensitivity reactions.

T-cell help (through production of interleukin-2) is also of great significance in cytotoxic component of delayed hypersensitivity.

Prevention and arrest of immune response traditionally is characterized as specific lymphocyte function called suppression.

Some subsets of CD8+ cells, CD4+ cells, as well as certain B-cells serve to suppress the immune responses. These regulatory functions are mediated for the most part by the secretion of soluble cytokines, but also through incomplete killing effect and even via anti-idiotypic autoantibodies. Both direct, antigen specific and non-direct antigen-specific suppression play an important part in regulation of immune response, in establishment of peripheral tolerance and control of autoimmunity (Fig. ). Classic concept of suppression mechanisms (M. Feldman, 1972; N.K. Damle, 1986) use to operate with the idea of special subset of T-suppressor lymphocytes, responsible exclusively for different (non-specific, idiotype-specific and antigen-specific) kinds of suppressing effect. Current concept denies the existence of lymphoid subset, specialized exclusively on suppression (A. Cook, 1994). In truth, suppressing functions are distributed between:

  • Th2 , which secret immunosuppressive cytokines;
  • CD8-positive cytotoxic lymphocytes, using incomplete killing effect for direct clonal suppression;
  • B-cells elaborating antiidiotypic autoantibodies (indirect idiotype-specific suppression). It has been demonstrated that small doses of antiidiotypes are able to trigger antigen-specific immune response, activating memory cells in the absence of an antigen itself. At the same time, their large doses suppress the antigen-specific immune response (K. Rajevsky, A. Takemori; 1983, A. Kulberg, 1986). CD8-positive cells are able to render suppressor action by means of free TcR secretion.

All the facts mentioned above witness that suppression as a phenomenon really exists, but suppressors as the separate from killers, helpers and B-cells subset of T-cells are mythical!

In response to specific antigen complementary to TcR or after non-specific boosting with some mitogens, T-cells are triggered into the process of activation. The peripheral T-cells are mostly long-living ones and stay in G0 or G1 phases of the cell cycle (Fig. , external greater circle). Upon activation by certain signals, they can bear one of the following functions: proliferation, differentiation, and cytokine production.

The complex of an antigen and HLA-protein, correspondent to the clonal TcR, achieves specific monoclonal activation of T-cells (as well as B-cells). This results in blast-transformation of T-lymphocytes with subsequent chain of cell divisions (8-10 times). In blast-transformation, G0 and G1 phases get exceedingly shortened S-periods and mitoses follow one after another (Fig. ; Internal smaller circle).

Non-specific polyclonal activation of T- (and also B-) lymphocytes is started by polyclonal activators or mitogens, such as plant lectins, concanavallin A or phytohemagglutinin. These agents bind the surface mitogenic receptors of lymphocytes, located on many various clones, so the response involves simultaneously a lot of different lymphoid clones. As a rule, polyclonal response is much weaker in comparison with regular monoclonal one, provoked by antigen-specific activation. Mitogenic stimulation, in difference with antigenic blast-transformation, does not trigger the serial cell divisions, but is limited with one mitosis.

Recently, a new class of T-cell activation messengers was discovered: so called “super-antigens”. Commonly, these are microbial products, but some foodstuff and prescribed drugs also belong to this group. The super-antigens are able to stimulate the T-cells carrying some variant of TcR in oligoclonal manner (Fig. ). Super-antigenicity is relative property: the same substance may be super-antigenic for one individual and indifferent for another. The mandatory attribute of a super-antigen is simultaneous correspondence between a portion of its molecule – and some unique variant of TcR, and between another portion of the same super-antigen molecule – and HLA class II glycoprotein (G.M. Johnson et al., 1992).

Super-antigens regardless of their TcR antigenic specificity, which is bypassed, activate cD4-positive lymphocytes. Activation of multiple T-clones by super-antigens results in massive production of various cytokines – interleukin-2, 6, 8; TNF, etc. These cytokines in greater doses display some systemic actions including fever, vomiting, diarrhea and even toxic-septic shock.

Sometimes, super-antigens, presented by B-lymphocytes activate T-helpers. In this situation there is parallel stimulation of B-lymphocytes themselves. Quite typically it is manifested in activation of autoreactive B-clones and precipitation of autoimmune syndromes.

The diversity of super-antigens includes gram-negative bacteria lipopolysaccharides, staphylococcus E enterotoxin, toxic shock syndrome toxin, Epstein - Barr virus and even components of kiwi fruit.

Super-antigens are of considerable importance for Pathophysiology of many allergoid reactions, including toxic dyspepsia in infants and food toxicoinfection in adults.

Biology Of The B Lymphocytes And Plasma Cells

Maturing of B-cells takes place both before and after birth. The bone marrow precursors of primitive B-cells express cytoplasmic m - chains of immunoglobulins, but their surface is free from antibody molecules. More differentiated, but still immature precursors of B-lymphocytes display both intact cytoplasmic and surface IgM. Mature B-cells have their cytoplasmic IgM lost, but still keep the surface one. In addition, they express surface IgD.

These changes develop under the influence of cytokines and are referred to as antigen-independent differentiation. Interestingly, the major part of functionally active antibody-secreting plasma cells is devoid of any surface immunoglobulins, but carry surface marker PC-1, up to date (1998) still not included into CD-system.

The B cell surface immunoglobulin can be identified by fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry after the cells have been incubated with anti-immunoglobulin antibodies labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate. This procedure is utilized clinically to identify and quantitate B cells. In addition, as with T cells, several monoclonal antibodies directed against B cell-surface molecules have been described. CD20 is ionic channel, specific distinctive marker of all B-cells. All B-cells also express globotriosyl-ceramide known as CD77. Some of the B cell-specific surface markers, such as CD19 (formerly termed B4), appear early in B-cell differentiation, prior to expression of surface immunoglobulin, and are therefore useful in determining the histogenesis of certain leukemias of childhood. It is essential for evaluation of clinical prognosis in lymphoblastic leukemia, because the forms originated from different stages of B-cell development have strikingly different percentage of remissions induced by the same treatment. For example, leukemia clones derived from early pre-B cells are more prone to treatment that those originated from later stages.

  • Mature B lymphocytes are present in the blood and lymphoid tissue, including bone marrow. In human blood they constitute 10 to 20% of the lymphocyte population. In lymph nodes they are found in the superficial cortex, forming lymphoid follicles. In the spleen they live in the white pulp organized as lymphoid follicles, usually having pale-staining central areas called germinal centers.

The genetic basis for the clonal diversity of the B-cell repertoire (i.e., the existence of numerous B cells programmed to recognize only one antigen) has been clarified by the studies that were initiated by S. Tonegava. There are remarkable similarities in the organization of immunoglobulin genes and genes of the T-cell receptor, discussed below (see “Immunoglobulins”).

Non-active mature B-cells can be stimulated for growth, hypertrophy of synthetic apparatus, division and secretion of antibodies. The triggers for this sequence of events may be different: specific (antigens) and non-specific (mitogens and super-antigens). In last case the mode of activation is polyclonal. As a rule, antigenic stimulation of B-lymphocytes requires not only an antigen with conformation determinant, correspondent to surface immunoglobulin receptor. The effective production of antibodies is achieved with participation of T-helpers. Antigen-specific T-helper clone expands after presentation of sequential antigen determinants by APC to T-cells (see above). Activated T-helpers secrete the cytokines (e.g. interleukin-2), which, along with specific antigen, precisely stimulate the blast-transformation and subsequent expansion of particular specific B-clone. This clone is transfigured into plasma cells, highly efficient factories of circulating antibodies.

In the course of immune response there occurs a swap of Ig-classes (isotypic switching). Initially, plasma cells produce IgM, later in primary response (or from the very beginning of boosted immune response) different isotypes other than IgM are prevailing. These may be IgG, IgA or IgE of the same paratopic specificity

It has been clearly shown that interleukin-4 and interleukin-13, produced by T-cells, are responsible for IgE synthesis, interleukin-5 promotes, at least along with other stimulants, the secretion of IgA; and g - interferon, as well as interleukin-12, decrease the generation of IgE. Some antigens contain multiple repeats of identical epitopes. Due to this, they are able to cross-link the surface immunoglobulin receptors of B-cells. As a result of such unusual mode of stimulation, B-lymphocytes may get activation without contribution of T-cells. This group of antigens is referred to as T-independent”. In fact, these are analogues of the mitogens, acting via mechanism very close to that described in previous section. The sense of their action over B-lymphocytes is, probably, explained by the evolutionary necessity to establish the response for bacterial challenge as early as possible. Among “T-independent” antigens are dextran, ficoll, some other bacterial polysaccharides, few proteins of Flagellata etc. Commonly, immune system rapidly responds for them with IgM and the strength of the reaction is not so great. Polyclonal stimulation of B-cells can be achieved also by Epstein-Barr virus, bound to CD21 marker of mature B-cells and follicular dendritic cells. This kind of polyclonal stimulation is responsible for the symptoms of infectious mononucleosis. Intensive production of cytokines causes high fever and activation of autoreactive B-clones leads to multi-systemic involvement in this disease and positive Paul-Bunnell test. The stimulated lymphocytes are enlarged, with big nuclei and considerable amount of basophilic cytoplasm. This unusual appearance was a reason to call them “virocytes” or in classic texts “lymphomonocytes” (I.A. Kassirsky), although they are purely lymphoid in their origin.

From the cellular level of discussion about the constituents of immune system, we can now dive deeper – to the molecular contributors of immunologic processes.

Immunoglobulins as markers and recognizing tools

The immunoglobulins (Ig) or antibodies, which is the same, are products of functionally active B-cells (plasma cells). (Fig ) They consist of polypeptide chains with varying configuration. There are 5 main classes, or isotypes of Ig; also subclasses are distinguished. Paul Ehrlich (1901) prophetically postulated the existence of antibodies as “circulating receptors separated from the surface of cell”. Soon it was discovered, that Ig belong to globulin fraction of blood serum and mostly migrate with its g - fraction (A. Tiselius, 1930-1948). R.R. Porter and J.M. Edelman (1957-1962) in general, decrypted the structure of Ig.

Ig molecules consist of polypeptides, joined together with disulfide bridges. They have characteristic spatial conformation and contain some carbohydrates, although non-protein moieties are not related to specific part of antibodies.

The main carcass of antibody consists of heavy (H) and light (L) chains. The example is IgG molecule, having two H- and two L- identical chains. Different portions of the whole Ig molecule serve for various functions. The portions with NH2-ends of heavy and light chains (Fab –Fragments antigen-binding) are variable, cause they are different in primary structure in antibodies of various specificity. Variable regions form a concave of unique shape, filled up with appropriate antigenic epitope. This end of molecule is used for binding of an antigen and bears the immunologic specificity of an antibody. Epitope-fixing area is peculiar in every antibody of a given specificity, and is called paratope. Paratope is, in turn, antigenic. The whole paratope, or its part, involved in immunologic interactions as an antigen is called idiotоpe, and the set of idiotopes located on specific antibody is known as idiotype. The variants of heavy chains antigenic structure, determined by allelic polymorphism of appropriate genes, which are characteristic for somatotype of certain groups of individuals, are called allotypes.

Hydroxyl COOH-end of Ig molecule is called Fc-portion (Fragment crystallizable or constant). It has the same structure in all antibodies of every particular class and subclass, but differs in Ig, belonging to different subclasses and classes (isotypic variance). Fc-portion fulfills different biological functions in various classes of Ig, making them tools for different purposes.

For instance, unique ability of IgG trespass the placental barrier depends on architecture of its Fc-portion. Fc-portion of Ig embodies the loci for binding of some important ligands: complement proteins, staphylococcal A and streptococcal G-proteins, receptors of macrophages and NK-cells (which is mandatory in phagocytosis and antibody-mediated cytotoxicity). So, this is a portion, essential for realization of antibody effector functions. Pepsin lyses Ig with liberation of (Fab)’2 и Fc-portions, and papain action gives two separate mono-Fab fragments and Fc-fragment together with mixture of short peptides – products of hinge region hydrolysis (R. Pumphrey). Every light and heavy chain is polypeptide, composed by means of transcription and translation of genes, encoding constant and variable portions of Ig. This is achieved through complex process of gene splicing (S. Tonegava, 1987-1991).

Antibody diversity is based on the fact that every individual B-lymphocyte and all its clonal descendents produce an immunoglobulin with unique spatial structure of paratope. Idiotypes never repeat. Variable portions of light and heavy chains are encoded by V-genes. This diversity could not be possible, if each particular Ig was encoded entirely by single unique large gene, from the very beginning till the end of its primary structure (like, for example, interleukins really are encoded).

S. Tonegava have demonstrated that heterogeneity of antibodies is achieved because different parts of every polypeptide in their molecules are encoded by different germ line genes, and each of this genes exists in genome as multi-gene, in numerous variants. Thus, in mice the immunoglobulin heavy chain is assembled from a pool of approximately 200 V, 12 D, and 4 J segments. Moreover, these multi-genes are objects of extremely frequent somatic mutation during ontogenesis of B-cells, which increases the degree of variance. The transposons – mobile dispersed portions of genome able to change their position in chromosomes during ontogenesis – are of greatest significance for Ig diversity generation (B. McClintock, 1983). The shadowed side of this effective “program files reshuffling” mechanism is relatively high risk of translocations, causing protooncogene expression in lymphoid cells This makes lymphomas and lymphogenous leukemias quite frequent among malignancies (6th place in incidence for North American population, according the statistics of 1997). . During B cell maturation there is somatic rearrangement of the DNA, as was described for T cells. Given the numbers of genes in the V,, D, and J regions for heavy chains and light chains, it is not difficult to estimate that antibodies with greater that 10 specificites can arise by unique combinatorial rearrangements of the immunoglobulin genes. As mentioned earlier with respect to T cells, it is important to note the practical application of this knowledge. Because in a given B cell and its progeny only one of the several million possible DNA rearrangements occurs, it is feasible to distinguish between monoclonal (neoplastic) and polyclonal (reactive or leukemoid) proliferations of B-cells by extracting the DNA and subjecting it to Southern blot analysis.

So, the structure of antibody molecule varies due to shuffle and splicing of m-RNA, obtained by means of alternating transcription of above-mentioned gene variants. There is persuasive hypothesis, that Ig diversity is a result of evolutionary divergence of few genes-precursors, initially, probably, encoded the enzymes glycosyltransferases (S. Roth; 1985). Besides alternating transcription of multi-gene portions and their somatic mutation, additional source of diversity is a drift of fusion point for polypeptides spliced (sutured) into single Ig molecule. The variants for L-chains are kept in chromosome 2 and 22, for H-chain – in chromosome 14.

For didactic purposes all these sources of antibodies diversity can be resumed as follows:

  • There are many alternative variants of germ line genes for variable areas of Ig L- and H-chains
  • Assembly of entire H-chain includes free recombination of products, encoded by one of many V, one of many J, and one of many D-regions, assembly of L-chain goes almost identically, but with one of numerous V and one of several J-region products, because the immunoglobulin light chain genes have a similar organization, except that they lack the D-region.
  • In recombination, fusion point drifts and is not precisely located, thus changing the structure of Ig.
  • V-genes of Ig are most mutable among all somatic genes, so 9 out of every 10 B-clones carry some mutant Ig-genes, absent in germ line native set.
  • Any H-chain can be combined with any L-chain.
  • In addition, there are eight constant region genes (Cm , Cg , Ca , and so on) corresponding to the immunoglobulin isotypes and their subclasses.

The same clone of B-cells can switch Ig biosynthesis from one isotype to another, not altering Ig antigenic specificity. This is achieved by poly-adenylation of primary transcript, containing RNA-copies of different C-genes in alternative points. Owing to this variation, transcript may loss in splicing different C-portions. This is regulated by cytokines and complement fragments and proceeds differently in non-boosted and boosted immune responses.

As a result of this complicated process, in ready Ig L-chains and H-chains both contain variable and constant areas, last ones are located in NH2-ends. There are light chains of 2 classes: k - and l -. Heavy chains are of 5 classes -g , a , m , d and e . These specifics correspond to 5 classes of Ig – G, A, M, D and E.

IgG – is a main monomer class of circulating Ig, migrating electrophoretically within its g -fraction. It is most active antiviral and antibacterial factor and potent opsonin, leading contributor of boosted immune response and single class of Ig, able to neutralize toxins. IgG is able yo penetrate across placental barrier and establish passive immunity in newborn for the period up to 3-6 months. Its half-life time in serum is 3 weeks. IgG, along with IgM activates classic pathway of complement system. There are 4 subclasses of IgG: IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4. They differ in small details of H-chain structure in Fc-portion. Antibodies of IgG2-subclass mostly function as opsonins and respond for toxins and dextrans. IgG1 and IgG2 in humans frequently account for anti-rhesus reactions. IgG4 in humans display significant affinity to mast cells and basophils, taking part in anaphylaxis, but IgG1 are completely devoid of anaphylactic potency. In rodents, for example, in guinea pig, the situation with IgG subclasses and anaphylaxis is exactly inverted.
IgA – in humans is presented in sera with monomer (80%) and dimer (20%). In other mammals dimeric form is predominant. IgA is specifically related to the functions of regional intro-barrier lymphoid systems associated with integument. It is main secretory product of lymphoid elements in respiratory, urogenital and gut mucosa. IgA also establish humoral immune defense in breast milk, colostrum, sweat and sebum, produced by skin glands. Secretory IgA, especially that of gut, has peculiar configuration, resistant to proteolysis. This form is dimeric, with special connective polypeptide chain, called secretory fragment. It is suggested that IgA is most effective against germ invasion via mucosa membranes. There are 2 subclasses of this antibody: IgA1 and IgA2. Electrophoretically it migrates not only with g - but also with b -globulin fractions. Immune complexes with IgA have quite slow clearance, which sometimes make them the contributors of immune complex disorders.
IgM or macroglobulin is a large molecule including 5 subunits, each comprised of  2 m - chains and 2 light chains. The subuhits form the vagon-wheel structure with Fc-portions looking inwards, bound together by connective chains. IgM presents not only in serum, but also in the cytoplasm and on the plasma membrane of immature B-cells. This is first class of antibodies, starting primary immune response. About 5 % of B-cells, those marked by CD5, produce spontaneously and after polyclonal activation autoreactive IgM of low affinity, directed towards non-tissue specific epitopes, like DNA, heat shock proteins and cytoskeleton elements- so called natural autoantibodies. Their excessive activity is blamed for some cases of autoimmune disorders. Unlike IgG, which is mostly serum Ig, macroglobulin distribution is balanced between blood and tissues. IgM is potent activator of complement classic pathway, highly efficient opsonin and agglutinating agent. Cold hemagglutinins, heterophilic antibodies and isohemagglutinins – all belong to this particular class of Ig. T-independent antigens trigger, preferentially, IgM-response. In controversy with traditional interpretation of Igs as g - globulin, IgM migrates in electrophoresis of blood serum with b - globulins and, partially, even with a 2 - globulins. Immune complexes with IgM in healthy person have relatively effective clearance.
IgD has 2 d -chains and two light ones. It is characteristic for the surface of immature B-cells and present in serum in trace low concentrations, although last is increased in polyclonal B-immunostimulation and paradoxally, in some innate immune deficits. The real significance of IgD is not yet completely explained. M. Owen and M. Stewart believe that IgD takes part in antigen-dependent differentiation of B-cells (1994).
IgE –  reagins or homocytotropic antibodies. Their classic function is to provoke hyperergic immunity and inflammation in response to macroparasites. The greater is their production in mammals and humans, the stronger is anti-helminthes resistance. They also play some physiological roles in placenta formation. IgE is blamed for atopic disorders and anaphylaxis. The major part of their unique properties is dictated by the presence of specific domain in Fc-portion. This domain is able to bind Fce R-I receptors of mast cells and basophils with high affinity. IgE-receptors of lower affinity (Fce R-2) may be expressed of the surface of other cells, later in course of anaphylactic reactions. Virtually all WBC, platelets, alveolar macrophages and (in patients with atopic dermatitis) also skin APC – Langerhans’ cells are able to express IgE-sensitive receptors under the influence of IL-4. When Fab-fragments of these receptor-bound IgE cross-link with a molecule of antigen, mast cell or another reagin-sensitized element starts degranulation and release of inflammation mediators, causing hyperergic anaphylactic reactions.

Small concentrations of IgE are present in tissues and blood plasma. The ability to produce small amount of IgE is controlled by dominant gene. Several different genes, controlling interleukin spectrum, interferon reception, T-helper behavior as well as genes of HLA, are associated with abnormally high IgE production. Increased synthesis of IgE is multifactorial sign, and its additive expression is influenced by many environmental factors. Individuals, predisposed to excessively high IgE production are so called hyperreactors. Their constitutional feature may manifest in wide variety of atopic diseases (hay fever, vasomotor rhinitis, bronchial asthma, Quinque’s oedema, atopic dermatitis and many others described in section “Allergy”).

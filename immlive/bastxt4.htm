The Cytokines: Communication Signals Of The Immune System

It is well known that the induction and regulation of immune responses involve signal interactions among cells of the immune system, many of which result from the release of soluble polypeptide and glycoprotein mediators of relatively small molecular mass. Their collective name is cytokines, to commemorate the fact, that these signals render their regulatory effects over not only lymphoid, but also virtually all somatic cells. They are non-related to the structure of concrete antigens, but antigens, recognized within immune system frequently drive their production. First cytokines were described by their activities over target cells as early as in 1957. Since 1969 the terms “lymphokines” and “monokines” by the source of mediators were coined. Later, in 1979 glycoprotein mediators of WBC interactions were called “interleukines” (IL). Up to date, about 40 IL are known. Among them, at least 20 are completely identified, cloned and sequenced. All cytokines function as autocrine (action on producing cell itself) and paracrine (action over cells in close vicinity) regulators. In high doses, obtained by polyclonal activation of immunocompetent cells, some of them may cause systemic effects. Unique feature of cytokines, as paracrine regulators, is related with permanent circulation of their target cells. Owing to this, large numbers of the cells can go through the zones of local cytokines production, get activated and spread all over the body. Not only antigen-induced immune response, but also phagocytosis of a pathogen and mitogen-induced stimulation of immune cells equally cause cytokine secretion.

Cytokines mediate their effects by binding to specific high-affinity receptors on their target cells. For example, IL-2 activates T-cells by binding to high-affinity IL-2 receptors (IL-2R). Blockade of the IL-2R by monoclonal antibodies directed against the receptor prevents T-cell activation. This observation provides a means by which T-cell activation, when undesirable (as in graft rejection), may be controlled.

Production of cytokines and responsiveness to cytokines are tightly regulated by environmental stimuli and other cytokines. For example, resting T cells produce little if any g - IFN; however, during cell-mediated immune response against tuberculosis mycobacteria, they produce this and many other cytokines that activate and immobilize macrophages, an action that is necessary for containing infection. Environmental influences on cytokine production may well be mediated by cytokines themselves; for example, a - TNF regulates the synthesis or release (or both) of IL-1 and hematopoetic growth factor by endothelial cells. Due to these permissive relations and tight intermingle, cytokines are regarded as regulatory net or cascade.

The knowledge gained about cytokines is not merely of academic interest; it has practical therapeutic ramifications as well. First, by regulating cytokine production or action it may be possible to control the harmful effects of inflammation, allergy or tissue-damaging immune reactions. Second, cytokines produced by recombinant DNA technology can be administered to enhance immunity against cancer or microbial infections (immunotherapy). Both these avenues are currently being pursued on an experimental basis in humans. The cytokines are characterized in special table below. Please notice that these regulators have multiple cellular sources and overlapped functions. For instance, IL-1, IL-6 and IL-8 are promoters of inflammation, IL-2 and IL-9 stimulate lymphocyte proliferation, IL-4, IL-5 and IL-12 are involved in isotypic switch of immunoglobulin classes, IL-10 serves as breaker of cytokine cascade, because it decreases the production of many other cytokines. IL-1, IL-6, IL-8 and TNF together are accountable for fever and the whole dynamics of acute phase response.

The integrated result of cytokine cascade activation includes organization of preimmune and immune responses, regulation of their strength and cytotoxic potential, as well as termination. Their contribution into the mechanisms of chronic inflammation, fever, blood clotting, acute phase response is great and decisive. Endocrine effects of cytokines are essential for stress dynamics and for genesis of emotions.

Interferons (IFN) is a group of versatile cytokines purified from supernatant fluid of cell cultures infected by viruses. The samples of these fluids, added to other cell cultures, inhibited the multiplication of different viruses. IFN are natural antiviral proteins of great interest for pathology, cause they represent signals triggering the defensive programs in viral cell injury. As virus itself is pathogenic program, IFN protection is based on pause of its reading and copying. Up to date, 3 classes of IFN are described: IFNs a ,b and g .

IFNa is a family of 20 peptides, produced by WBC. IFNb is one polypeptide elaborated by fibroblasts and epitheliocytes. Both IFNs significantly diminish the virus replication and display antiproliferative potency against certain cells (renal cancer, leukemic blasts in hairy cell leukemia). Both IFNs are highly homologic in structure, encoded in 6th chromosome and act on the same receptor. According A. Inglot (1983), the signal for the start of cellular IFN production is a cell contact with virions, their fragments, double-strand RNA, non-viral intracellular parasites and artificial inducers (polyvinyl-sulfate, imudane etc). IFN bound cellular receptors and internalize into target cells. This leads to intracellular synthesis of prostaglandines and leukotriens. The equation c-GMP/ c-AMP increases. As a result, infected cell sharply decrease the synthesis of viral m-RNA and proteins. The cell expresses the enzyme 2’-5’-adenylatsynthetase, which is activated by double-strand RNA and produces 2’-5’-oligoadenilates, facilitating the destruction of viral RNA by another enzyme, endonuclease. As 2’-5’-oligoadenilates are very prone to decay under the influence of phosphodiestherase, the effect of IFN is relatively short-termed. IFNs also activate proteinkinase, phosphorylating the ribosomal proteins eIF-2 and P1. This causes their inactivation and general decrease of protein translation within the cell, including the halt of viral protein production.

IFNs inhibit methylation of viral RNA, essential for its transcription; derange the function of t-RNAs, and in some cells imitate the action of antibiotic tunicamycin – obstacle the glycosylation and self-assemblage of virions. All kinds of cytotoxicity are promoted by IFNs. These regulators stimulate the expression of HLA-proteins and thus promote antigen presentation. Anti-tumor efficacy of IFNs results not only from their anti-viral and cytotoxic action, but also is based upon their ability to restore cytoskeleton structure, activate collagen and fibronectin biosynthesis, inhibit cellular growth, refresh adhesive properties of the cells and stop metastases activity

However, it is worth to remind, that J.-F. Bottazzo et al. have proven the role of prescribed IFNs as provocative agents and risk factors for autoallergy. The risk of Hashimoto’s thyroiditis is several times greater in patients treated by means of interferonotherapy for chronic viral hepatitis and cancer. The reason for this untowards effect is aberrant expression of HLA-II molecules on thyroid cells, complicated by autopresentation of their antigens, boosted by IFNs.

IFNg has less potent antiviral activity, but represents greatest ability to promote HLA-I and –II expression and cytotoxic functions. It has nothing common with other IFNs in structure and is encoded by different chromosome (9th). So, its name IFN is more willingly traditional than generic. This cytokine is secreted by T-cells, both active and calm, mostly it is associated with T-helpers of 1st type and NC-cells. The properties of this molecule are described in the table above. It is believed, that hereditary posttranscriptional defect of IFNg secretion can be most important reason for anaphylaxis and atopic diseases.

As the receptor of IFNa b is encoded by 21st chromosome, Down’s syndrome with trisomy 21 is characterized by excess of receptors and higher sensitivity to this cytokines. It is manifested in lymphopenia, early accidental thymus atrophy, immune deficiency, autoimmune disorders and increased incidence of leukemia in Down patients. The blood level of IFNs is compensatory decreased in them.

A lot of colony-stimulating factors (CSFs), like granulocytic (G-CSF), or granolocytic-monocytic (GM-CSF) also are derived from T-cells. These cytokines are accountable for the interactions between immune and hematopoetic systems. They cause leukocytosis and shifts of granulocyte nuclear formula, characteristic for inflammation and immunopathologic reactions. For example, G-CSF and GM-CSF regulate the bone marrow production of granulocytes and monocytes, giving the option of T-cell controlling interference into the course of inflammation and acute phase response.

Many other cytokines – transforming growth factors (TGFs), tumot necrosis factors (TNFs), leukemia inhibiting factor (LIF), migration inhibition factor (MIF) play important roles in different cell activation and inhibition, anti-tumor defense, cell growth, fibroplasia, fever and wound healing, tissue repair.

Histocompability Proteins And Their Role In Immunity

Although originally identified as antigens that evoke rejection of transplanted organs, histocompatibility antigens are now considered important in the regulation of the immune responce, as well as in resistance or susceptibility to a growing list of diseases. The histocompability antigens (and the corresponding genes) are complex in structure. These are highly polymorphic membrane glycoproteins, serving as markers of self cells. They take part in the formation of the antigen-containing complex, recognized by lymphocytes during immune response. In this role, histocompatibility proteins are able to influence the orientation and the very choice of epitopes, presented to lymphocytes. Of course, this is essential for distinguish between self and alien antigens and for determination of strength and character of immune response. The system is highly polymorphic - that is, there are several alternative forms (alleles) of a gene of each locus. This constitutes a formidable barrier in organ transplantation and is displayed in broad group diversity of human reactivity (B. Benacerraf, J.D. Snell and J. Dosse; Nobel Prize in Medicine in 1980).

It is well known that when an individual receives an organ transplant obtained from a genetically dissimilar donor, the transplanted organ is rejected by immunologic mechanisms. In this process of rejection the recepient's immune system recognizes the histocompability antigens displayed on the cells surfaces of the donor organ. Several genes code for histocompability antigens, but those that code for the most important transplantation antigens are clustered on a small segment of chromosome 6. This cluster of genes constitutes the human major histocompability complex (MHC) and is also known as the HLA complex. It is equivalent to the murine H-2 complex. Because HLA antigens form an allelic series, an individual inherits only one determinant from each parent and can have no more than two different antigens for every locus. Thus, cells of a heterozygous individual will express six different class I HLA antigens, three of maternal origin and three of paternal origin. Owing to the polymorphism at the major HLA loci, innumerable combinations of antigen can exist, ant therefore each individual in a noninbred population is likely to have a more or less unique antigenic profile, like a fingerprint on the cell surface.

The initials HLA stand for human leukocyte antigens, as MHC-encoded antigens were initially detected on the white cells. But, in fact HLA antigens of class I present on all nucleated cells and some postcellular structures, e.g. on platelets. Class II HLA-antigens are peculiar to immunocometent cells, including the whole spectrum of APC, B-cells and active T-cells; but principally can be induced on many other somatic cells (endothelium, endocrinocytes, tubular epithelium etc.) by means of appropriate cytokine signals.

MHC is a crucial link of immune regulation. Its role in immune response became clear as a result of fundamental research of 1974-1978 by P.C. Doherty and R.M. Zinkernagel (Nobel prize of 1996 in Medicine).

Based on their chemical structure, tissue distribution, and function, the MHC gene products are classified into three categories:

Class I antigens are coded by three closely linked loci designated HLA-A, HLA-C and also by non-classic loci titled E, F and G. Each of these molecules is a heterodimer, consisting of a big a - chain (polymorphic glycoprotein of 45,000 daltons or heavy chain), linked noncovalently to a smaller nonpolymorphic peptide called b 2-microglobulin. The latter is encoded by a gene on chromosome 15. Class I antigens are plasma membrane-associated glycoproteins, present on virtually all nucleated cells and platelets. Heave chain has variable portions, remote from plasma membrane, and constant ones, close to the cell surface. Long variable sequences of heavy chain is a product of co-dominantly inherited from both parents genes of A, B and C loci.

These molecules take part in recoghition of the antigens produced within APC or endocellular (do not confuse with endogenous of with self antigens).

They are recognized by CD8-positive cytotoxic lymphocytes .

 

An antigen, originated within APC (regardless of its self or alien nature – for example, an autoantigen or equally the antigens of viral and parasitic origin), is processed by special complex of non-lysosomal proteases called proteasome.

In rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER), APC synthetize MHC Class I proteins, later they bind the fragments of endocellular antigens, coming from proteasomes. This process requires the set of special carriers – ABC-proteins. The complex of endocellular antigen with MHC-I peptide goes into Golgi apparatus and later is transferred on the surface membrane for its presentation to CD8+-cytotoxic T-lymphocytes. Presentation occures in special groove on the surface of the cellular receptor and requires direct contact of a lymphocyte and APC , thus allowing some kind of comparision between self MHC peptide and presented antigen sequence (traditional name for this process – double recognition or recognition of an antigen “in context of MHC I protein”).

Class II antigens are coded for in a region known as HLA-D. The were initially defined by a phenomenon called the mixed lymphocyte reaction, which occurs when lymphocytes from two individuals, who have differing HLA-D regions, are cultured together in vitro. T lymphocytes from one individual recognize ("see") foreign class II antigens on the cells of the other individual and undergo proliferation. If there is genetic identity at the HLA-D region, proliferation does not occur. Recent studies have identified three subregions, DP, DQ, and DR, within the originally defined HLA-D region. Certain class II antigens cannot be detected by the mixed lymphocyte reaction and are designated HLA-D antigens, to distinguish them from the serologically defined D-region antigens, such as HLA -DR and HLA-DQ. However, unlike the case with DP, DQ, and DR loci, there is no single definable special HLA-D locus within the HLA-D region. It is believed that the responding T cells in the mixed lymphocyte reaction are detecting an array of HLA-DP, HLA-DQ, and/or HLA-DR antigens, some of which are not serologically detectable. Class II antigens differ from class I antigens in several respects.

They contain two chains, covalently linked (a - and b - with molecular weights of 34.000 daltons and 29.000 daltons, respectively). Both are highly polymorphic and resemble class I a - chains, also they have constant areas.

Their tissue expression is more limited than in class I antigens. They are normal constituents of APC’s, B-cell’s and activated T-cell’s plasma membrane. But, the phenomenon of induced of abberant expression of class II molecules on somatic cells occurs very frequently, under pathologic conditions and in response to various antigenic challenges. Abnormal expression of class II MHC glycoproteins on somatic cells is responcible for many cases of autoimmune disorders (for instance, autoimmune thyroid diseases), because a cell, expressing MHC II may start to present its own antigens to lymphocytes.

Normally these MHC-proteins are obligatory participants in recognition of the antigens, exocellular for APC. Exocellular does not necessarily mean that these antigens are alien. It means simply that they were engulfed or trapped by APC from around, by means of endocytosis. These antigens have a traffic within APC, which is somewhat different from that of intracellularly produced ones.

Class II molecules are also synthetized within RER, then go into Golgi complex, but ABC-proteins do not bind them. Owing to this, class II MHCmolecules are not able to attach antigenic fragments of endocellular origin. Instead of this, they temporarily connect with the invariant peptide Ii , coded for non-MHC genes. Ii-peptide is a kind of provisional bung, utilized for separation of intracellular class I and class II migration routes. Clas II molecules go into some endocytotic vesicles ( during inflammation and antigenic challenge - quite often, into phagolysosomes of mononuclear phagocytes). There they meet the fragments of engulfed and APC-processed exocellular (not always alien) antigens. Sequence fragment of an antigen ousts the Ii-peptide out of the complex, and new complex of antigen and class II protein goes into plasma membrane. These complex is a subject of interaction (according traditional vocabulary - “double recognition”) with CD4-positive T-regulators (helpers/inducers). The whole chain of events, described above is known.

So, individual amino-acide composition of class II and class I proteins may determine the character and the position of presented antigen fragments, as well as more or less precise manner and results of distinguishing between self and non-self sequences. It is attractive to propose, that the probability of mistakes in recognition (and, hence, the risk of autoimmune disorders, provoked by concrete antigenic challenge in different persons, as well as individual strength of immune response against a given germ) will depend on inherited individual’s MHC haplotype. Epidemiology and medical genetics both have obtained the data, supporting this hypothesis. This opic is discussed later as “MHC and diseases association”.

Both class I and class II MHC molecules, together with TcR, Fc-portions of Igs, secretory fragment of IgA, CD 2,3,4,8 – receptors and some other recognizing proteins belong to superfamily of Ig-genes and their conficuration is close to typical that of antibody molecule. They were originated from common evolutionary progenitor gene and bear some typical features, like multiple polypeptide chains and disulfide briges.

The “class III MHC molecules” are non-homologous to the members of this superfamily. This is traditional name for some humoral effectors of immune system, encoded in 6th chromosome between the genes of MHC I and II.

These are the genes code for complement system components: properdin, C2, C4, and Bf. Besides, here one can found the coding gene for important cytokines - TNF-a and TNF-b . They do not act as histocompatibility (transplantation) antigens, but also are polymorphic. For example, properdin has 4 alternative alleles. The degree (character) of class III antigens’ expression also contribute into immunological individuality of a person. It has been proven, that low expression of several complement factors makes a person predisposed to immune complex reactions and risky for different associated syndromes (vasculitis, glomerulonephritis, arthritis, uveitis). In patients with systemic lupus erythematosus and their close relatives quite frequently occurs heterozygous carriership of null-alleles for C2, C4 and C4B. In absense or in deficiency of these complement fragments, their opsonising function for immune-complex phagocytosis is deranged. Due to this, the clearance of immune complexes, normally produced in everyone after any humoral immune response and engufed by phagocytes, is considerably delayed in persons with complement factors’ deficit. Not extinguished properly by phagocytes, immune complexes get the chance to sediment over vascular wall or cumulate within tissues – and trigger thrombosis and inflammation, damaging self organs (Fig. ).

As already mentioned, a feature shared by class I and class II genes is the high degree of polymorphism. So, each of the several alleles at these loci is designated by a number, such as HLA-A1, HLA-B5, and so forth. (Those alleles that have not been fully characterized are identified by a W (workshop) - e.g., HLA-BW4.) All class I determinants evoke the formation of humoral antibodies in genetically nonindentical individuals. This makes it possible to type these antigens by conventional serologic techniques such as antibody-and complement-mediated lysis. Sera of multiparous women (who are immunized by the paternal antigens of the fetus) have traditionally served as a useful source of such anti-HLA antibodies. The presense of moderate maternal immune response against paternal HLA-I antigens of fetus is essential for appropriate formation of placenta in humans. Hence, placentogenesis looks like a kind of highly specialized productive inflammation induced, by physiological immune conflict between mother and fetus, interpreted by maternal immune system as a kind of tolerable parasite. If parents are close or near-identical in HLA haplotype, placenta formation does not proceed intensively and the resulting placenta is less functional. This phenomenon is the immunological component for the explanation of well-known fact: decreased fetus survival and greater incidense of miscarriage in inbriding. Moreover, the necessity of alien paternal components in fetal haplotype for placenta formation makes the true parthenogenesis in higher mammals impossible.

Now monoclonal anti-HLA antibodies, obtained by immunizing rodents with human cells, are available for diagnostic purposes.

Practical Significance Of MHC Complex

Medical importance of MHC-system is triple. It is related to graft transplantation, to problem of allergy and autoimmunity and to association with certaine immunopathologic and non-immunopathologic diseases.

As stated earlier, HLA antigens were discovered in the course of transplantation studies, and they continue to present main obstacle to the success of clinical organ transplantation. HLA antigens of the graft evoke both humoral and cell-mediated responses, which lead eventually to graft destruction. Because the severity of the rejection reaction is related to the degree of HLA disparity between the donor and recipient, HLA typing is of immense clinical significance in the selection of the donor-recipient combinations.

Class I and II HLA antigens play important but distinct roles in the induction and expression of cellular as well as humoral immunity. Class antigens are involved primarilly in regulating the function of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, which are believed to be important in resistance against virus infections. It can be demonstrated experimentally that cytotoxic T cells specifically sensitized against a virus can recognize and lyse only those virus-infected cells that bear self class I molecules. The basis of this phenomenon of HLA restriction seems to be that class I HLA antigens provide specific sites that are modified by virus infection and constitute the target antigen for the T cell receptors of the cytotoxic (CD8+) T cells. Because virus-infected cells can be recognized only in the context of class I MHC antigens, it makes "good sense" to have widespread expression of class I HLA antigens.

Class II antigens are particularly important in cell-to-cell interactions, especially in the activation of CD4+ T cells. In contrast to CD8+ killer T cells that recognize antigens in association with class I MHC antigens, CD4+ helper T cells recognize antigens only in the context of class II antigens on the surface of macrophages or other antigen-presenting cells. The role of class II antigens in the induction of T helper cells may also have an important bearing on the genetic regulation of the immune response. It is well known that the magnitude of immune responses is controlled by the genes that map within the HLA-D region, previously calld Ir-genes and now proven to be class II. How class II antigens influence the magnitude of the immune response is not fully understood. It is conceivable that certain class II allels may code for cell-surface (class II) molecules that form highly immunogenetic complexes with a given antigen. This in turn would translate into a vigorous activation of T-helper cells and a strong immune response. The consequence of inheriting such a class II gene would depend upon the nature of the antigen and the type of immune response generated. For example, if the antigen were ragweed pollen and the response were production of IgE antibody, the individual would be genetically prone to attcks of sneezing. on the other hand, good responsiveness to influenza virus may well mean a sniffle-free winter. Anyway, if doctor observes the cases of unusual strength of immune response, causing autoimmune disease or allergy, as well as cases of unique immunological weakness, responsible for relinquishment of some individuals against concrete germ strains, - it is necessary to stress that these phenomena may result from individual MHC haplotype specifics.

A variety of diseases have been found in association with certain HLA types (H. Matej, J. Batchelor, 1984). The best known is the association between ankylosing spondylites and HLA-B27; individuals who possess HLA-B27 antigen have a 87-fold greater chance (relative risk) of developing this disease than those who are negative for HLA-B27, and for Reiter’s syndrome relative risk factor in B27 carriers is 37! The diseases that show association with HLA can be broadly grouped into the following categories: (1) immunopathologic inflammatory deseases, including ankylosing spondylitis and several postinfectious arthropathies, all associated with HLA-B27; (2) organospecific autoimmune diseases, like autoimmune endocrinopathies, associated with alleles at the DR locus; (3) non-immunological monogenic inherited errors of metabolism. and (4) complement deficiency syndromes, causing immune-complex disorders.

Concerning 1) and 2) categories, the examples of clinically significant association of certain HLA-alleles and some diseases’ high risk are numerous and striking. HLA D3 and D4 genes are present in as few as 4% of Caucasians, but among white patients with insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (IDDM), which is organ-specific cytotoxic autoimmune disease, D3 either D4 (or both) are encounted in 90% of cases! Mutual presense of these two antigens makes the risk of IDDM 14,3 greater. There is an opinion, that D3 predispose to autoallergy due to decrease of lymphocyte suppression function in its holders. D4 is blamed for decreasing the resistanse against pancreatotropic viruses ( Coxsackie B4, ECHO, german measles and mumps). However, alternative hypothesis stresses that increased risk of IDDM in DR3-4 carriers is related not to D3 and D4 themselves, but to alleles linked with them and belonging to DQ-group. The presence in 53rd position of DQ-protein’s b - chain any other amino-acid, except aspartate, elevates the risk of IDDM. DQ3-allele is linked with higher risk of celiakia.

The allele DR3 is very often expressed in association with different autoimmune disorders, manifested with antibodies towards cellular receptors and/or in autoimmune endicrinopathies. Not infrequently, DR3 is combined with B8 and A2 class I antigens. The risk of Addison’s disease, which is commonly caused by chronic autoimmune adrenalitis, is 7 times greater amongst the bearers of DR3-antigen. D. Fostman et al (1991) proposed that the increased risk of autoimmune endocrine diseases, including IDDM and disorders of thyroid and adrenal glands, can be related to some inherited defect of intracellular carrier ABC-protein, taking part in antigen presentation by MHC-class I molecules.

B27 gene is associated with increased risk of disorders manifested in non-organospecific autoallergy (autoimmune diseases of connective tissue).

There is a clear linkage between DR3 and DR5 genes and autoimmune thyropathies, including Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (relative risk factor of about 3,5). Last gene is also related to increased risk of autoimmune aplastic anemia, namely the form, provoked in it’s carriers by parvovirus B19 infection. DR2 carriers are highly risky for Goodpasture’s syndrome (relative risk quotient 15,9) and multiple sclerosis (4,1).

DR4 gene is associated with higher risk of myasthenia gravis (autoimmune block of N-cholinergic receptors) and also with rheumatoid arthritis. Juvenile form of last disease (Still’s syndrome)is related to DR5-antigen.

There is a linkage between gene B8 and atopic diseases, gene B5 – and Behcet’s disease, as well as duodenal peptic ulcer. It has been proven, that different persons vary in intensity of anti-parasitic immunity depending on their MHC-haplotype.

Speaking about 3) category, we mean the inborn diseases caused by defective genes, occasionally located close to some of MHC-alleles, but, probably irrelevant to immunopathologic mechanisms. A typical example is the gene of primary hemochromatosis, wide spread in european populations, especially among French. It is located also in 6th chromosome, in its short arm, close to MHC class I genes and display obvious linkage disequilibrium with A3 (and, to lesser extent also with B7 , B14). Similar non-immunologic linkage exists between B47 and congenital adrenal cortex hyperplasia (caused by 21-steroid-hydroxilase deficiency and especially common among Eskimos). There is a relation between B35 – and subacute thyroiditis, B47 – and psoriasis. The immunopathologic nature of last two diseases is strongly suspeed, but not yet proven.

As a kind of resume for the discussion of this hot problem, we mast conclude, that the mechanisms underlying associations between MHC and disease are different and only partially understood at present. The following two mechanisms have been proposed.

Involvement of immune response genes. It was mentioned previously that HLA class II genes can regulate immune responsiveness. Accordingly, an association between certain autoimmune diseases and HLA-DR antigens may result from an exaggerated immune response to autoantigens.

Direct participation of HLA macromolecules in disease. There are two possible mechanisms by which HLA molecules may participate directly in disease. First, pathogens may share a cross-reacting antigen with HLA and be protected from an immune response by the host's tolerance for self-HLA antigens ("molecular mimicry"). This in effect would provide the pathogen an "immunologic permit" to invade and destroy tissues at will.

Alternatively, a strong immune response may be mounted against the invading pathogen, but because of the sharing of determinants between the microbe and the HLA antigen, immunologically mediated damage may be spread over normal tissues.

Second, certain HLA molecules may provide receptors for viruses, and this may either facilitate virus-cell interaction or provide a target for host immune cells to destroy virus-infected cells. In the former case, the presence of a given HLA type would favor virus-induced disease, whereas in the latter case it would favor removal of virus-infected cells.

A student, of course, understands that certain MHC haplotypes do not automatically make their host sick and are of no relation with the attempts to separate the mankind on sorts or strata.

For realization of probability, created by MHC specifics, an individual needs to undergo the action of some trigger cause, as a rule – some kind of exogenous antigenic provocation. This challenge is not always infectious. The presense of some MHC-proteins is a prerequisite of certain drug-induced allergic and autoimmune reactions. For example, hydralazine triggers lupus-like syndrome in carriers of DR4, the haplotypes DR3 and DR5 predispose to systemic sclerosis after the contact with vinyl-chloride, haplotypes A1, B8, Cw7 and DR3 are common for patients treated with colloid gold preparations and suffering from jatrogenic adverse nephropathy. The relations of this kind are of utmost interest for clinical pharmacology.

Anyway, association of MHC-genes and diseases makes concrete the classic concept of constitutio corporis, known from ancient times, and gives definite molecular critheria to the relationship between human constitution and diseases, empirically noticed long ago.

Conclusion

Having reviewed the fundamentals of BASIC IMMUNOLOGY, we can now turn to general disorders of the immune system and some specific immunologic diseases. These will be discussed under four broad headings: (1) hypersensitivity reactions, which form the mechanisms of immunologic injury in a variety of diseases, sphere of ALLERGOLOGY ; (2) autoimmune diseases, which are caused by immune reactions against self, sphere of AUTOIMMUNITY; and (3) immunologic deficiency syndromes, which result from relatively distinct or complex, genetically determined or acquired defects in some coomponents of the normal immune response, discussed in the section of immunodeficiencies.

 

The Cytokines: Communication Signals Of The Immune System

It is well known that the induction and regulation of immune responses involve signal interactions among cells of the immune system, many of which result from the release of soluble polypeptide and glycoprotein mediators of relatively small molecular mass. Their collective name is cytokines, to commemorate the fact, that these signals render their regulatory effects over not only lymphoid, but also virtually all somatic cells. They are non-related to the structure of concrete antigens, but antigens, recognized within immune system frequently drive their production. First cytokines were described by their activities over target cells as early as in 1957. Since 1969 the terms “lymphokines” and “monokines” by the source of mediators were coined. Later, in 1979 glycoprotein mediators of WBC interactions were called “interleukines” (IL). Up to date, about 40 IL are known. Among them, at least 20 are completely identified, cloned and sequenced. All cytokines function as autocrine (action on producing cell itself) and paracrine (action over cells in close vicinity) regulators. In high doses, obtained by polyclonal activation of immunocompetent cells, some of them may cause systemic effects. Unique feature of cytokines, as paracrine regulators, is related with permanent circulation of their target cells. Owing to this, large numbers of the cells can go through the zones of local cytokines production, get activated and spread all over the body. Not only antigen-induced immune response, but also phagocytosis of a pathogen and mitogen-induced stimulation of immune cells equally cause cytokine secretion.

Cytokines mediate their effects by binding to specific high-affinity receptors on their target cells. For example, IL-2 activates T-cells by binding to high-affinity IL-2 receptors (IL-2R). Blockade of the IL-2R by monoclonal antibodies directed against the receptor prevents T-cell activation. This observation provides a means by which T-cell activation, when undesirable (as in graft rejection), may be controlled.

Production of cytokines and responsiveness to cytokines are tightly regulated by environmental stimuli and other cytokines. For example, resting T cells produce little if any g - IFN; however, during cell-mediated immune response against tuberculosis mycobacteria, they produce this and many other cytokines that activate and immobilize macrophages, an action that is necessary for containing infection. Environmental influences on cytokine production may well be mediated by cytokines themselves; for example, a - TNF regulates the synthesis or release (or both) of IL-1 and hematopoetic growth factor by endothelial cells. Due to these permissive relations and tight intermingle, cytokines are regarded as regulatory net or cascade.

The knowledge gained about cytokines is not merely of academic interest; it has practical therapeutic ramifications as well. First, by regulating cytokine production or action it may be possible to control the harmful effects of inflammation, allergy or tissue-damaging immune reactions. Second, cytokines produced by recombinant DNA technology can be administered to enhance immunity against cancer or microbial infections (immunotherapy). Both these avenues are currently being pursued on an experimental basis in humans. The cytokines are characterized in special table below. Please notice that these regulators have multiple cellular sources and overlapped functions. For instance, IL-1, IL-6 and IL-8 are promoters of inflammation, IL-2 and IL-9 stimulate lymphocyte proliferation, IL-4, IL-5 and IL-12 are involved in isotypic switch of immunoglobulin classes, IL-10 serves as breaker of cytokine cascade, because it decreases the production of many other cytokines. IL-1, IL-6, IL-8 and TNF together are accountable for fever and the whole dynamics of acute phase response.

The integrated result of cytokine cascade activation includes organization of preimmune and immune responses, regulation of their strength and cytotoxic potential, as well as termination. Their contribution into the mechanisms of chronic inflammation, fever, blood clotting, acute phase response is great and decisive. Endocrine effects of cytokines are essential for stress dynamics and for genesis of emotions.

Interferons (IFN) is a group of versatile cytokines purified from supernatant fluid of cell cultures infected by viruses. The samples of these fluids, added to other cell cultures, inhibited the multiplication of different viruses. IFN are natural antiviral proteins of great interest for pathology, cause they represent signals triggering the defensive programs in viral cell injury. As virus itself is pathogenic program, IFN protection is based on pause of its reading and copying. Up to date, 3 classes of IFN are described: IFNs a ,b and g .

IFNa is a family of 20 peptides, produced by WBC. IFNb is one polypeptide elaborated by fibroblasts and epitheliocytes. Both IFNs significantly diminish the virus replication and display antiproliferative potency against certain cells (renal cancer, leukemic blasts in hairy cell leukemia). Both IFNs are highly homologic in structure, encoded in 6th chromosome and act on the same receptor. According A. Inglot (1983), the signal for the start of cellular IFN production is a cell contact with virions, their fragments, double-strand RNA, non-viral intracellular parasites and artificial inducers (polyvinyl-sulfate, imudane etc). IFN bound cellular receptors and internalize into target cells. This leads to intracellular synthesis of prostaglandines and leukotriens. The equation c-GMP/ c-AMP increases. As a result, infected cell sharply decrease the synthesis of viral m-RNA and proteins. The cell expresses the enzyme 2’-5’-adenylatsynthetase, which is activated by double-strand RNA and produces 2’-5’-oligoadenilates, facilitating the destruction of viral RNA by another enzyme, endonuclease. As 2’-5’-oligoadenilates are very prone to decay under the influence of phosphodiestherase, the effect of IFN is relatively short-termed. IFNs also activate proteinkinase, phosphorylating the ribosomal proteins eIF-2 and P1. This causes their inactivation and general decrease of protein translation within the cell, including the halt of viral protein production.

IFNs inhibit methylation of viral RNA, essential for its transcription; derange the function of t-RNAs, and in some cells imitate the action of antibiotic tunicamycin – obstacle the glycosylation and self-assemblage of virions. All kinds of cytotoxicity are promoted by IFNs. These regulators stimulate the expression of HLA-proteins and thus promote antigen presentation. Anti-tumor efficacy of IFNs results not only from their anti-viral and cytotoxic action, but also is based upon their ability to restore cytoskeleton structure, activate collagen and fibronectin biosynthesis, inhibit cellular growth, refresh adhesive properties of the cells and stop metastases activity

However, it is worth to remind, that J.-F. Bottazzo et al. have proven the role of prescribed IFNs as provocative agents and risk factors for autoallergy. Histocompability Proteins And Their Role In Immunity

Although originally identified as antigens that evoke rejection of transplanted organs, histocompatibility antigens are now considered important in the regulation of the immune responce, as well as in resistance or susceptibility to a growing list of diseases. The histocompability antigens (and the corresponding genes) are complex in structure. These are highly polymorphic membrane glycoproteins, serving as markers of self cells. They take part in the formation of the antigen-containing complex, recognized by lymphocytes during immune response. In this role, histocompatibility proteins are able to influence the orientation and the very choice of epitopes, presented to lymphocytes. Of course, this is essential for distinguish between self and alien antigens and for determination of strength and character of immune response. The system is highly polymorphic - that is, there are several alternative forms (alleles) of a gene of each locus. This constitutes a formidable barrier in organ transplantation and is displayed in broad group diversity of human reactivity (B. Benacerraf, J.D. Snell and J. Dosse; Nobel Prize in Medicine in 1980).

It is well known that when an individual receives an organ transplant obtained from a genetically dissimilar donor, the transplanted organ is rejected by immunologic mechanisms. In this process of rejection the recepient's immune system recognizes the histocompability antigens displayed on the cells surfaces of the donor organ. Several genes code for histocompability antigens, but those that code for the most important transplantation antigens are clustered on a small segment of chromosome 6. This cluster of genes constitutes the human major histocompability complex (MHC) and is also known as the HLA complex. It is equivalent to the murine H-2 complex. Because HLA antigens form an allelic series, an individual inherits only one determinant from each parent and can have no more than two different antigens for every locus. Thus, cells of a heterozygous individual will express six different class I HLA antigens, three of maternal origin and three of paternal origin. Owing to the polymorphism at the major HLA loci, innumerable combinations of antigen can exist, ant therefore each individual in a noninbred population is likely to have a more or less unique antigenic profile, like a fingerprint on the cell surface.

The initials HLA stand for human leukocyte antigens, as MHC-encoded antigens were initially detected on the white cells. But, in fact HLA antigens of class I present on all nucleated cells and some postcellular structures, e.g. on platelets. Class II HLA-antigens are peculiar to immunocometent cells, including the whole spectrum of APC, B-cells and active T-cells; but principally can be induced on many other somatic cells (endothelium, endocrinocytes, tubular epithelium etc.) by means of appropriate cytokine signals.

MHC is a crucial link of immune regulation. Its role in immune response became clear as a result of fundamental research of 1974-1978 by P.C. Doherty and R.M. Zinkernagel (Nobel prize of 1996 in Medicine).

Based on their chemical structure, tissue distribution, and function, the MHC gene products are classified into three categories:

Class I antigens are coded by three closely linked loci designated HLA-A, HLA-C and also by non-classic loci titled E, F and G. Each of these molecules is a heterodimer, consisting of a big a - chain (polymorphic glycoprotein of 45,000 daltons or heavy chain), linked noncovalently to a smaller nonpolymorphic peptide called b 2-microglobulin. The latter is encoded by a gene on chromosome 15. Class I antigens are plasma membrane-associated glycoproteins, present on virtually all nucleated cells and platelets. Heave chain has variable portions, remote from plasma membrane, and constant ones, close to the cell surface. Long variable sequences of heavy chain is a product of co-dominantly inherited from both parents genes of A, B and C loci.

These molecules take part in recoghition of the antigens produced within APC or endocellular (do not confuse with endogenous of with self antigens).

They are recognized by CD8-positive cytotoxic lymphocytes .

 

An antigen, originated within APC (regardless of its self or alien nature – for example, an autoantigen or equally the antigens of viral and parasitic origin), is processed by special complex of non-lysosomal proteases called proteasome.

In rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER), APC synthetize MHC Class I proteins, later they bind the fragments of endocellular antigens, coming from proteasomes. This process requires the set of special carriers – ABC-proteins. The complex of endocellular antigen with MHC-I peptide goes into Golgi apparatus and later is transferred on the surface membrane for its presentation to CD8+-cytotoxic T-lymphocytes. Presentation occures in special groove on the surface of the cellular receptor and requires direct contact of a lymphocyte and APC , thus allowing some kind of comparision between self MHC peptide and presented antigen sequence (traditional name for this process – double recognition or recognition of an antigen “in context of MHC I protein”).

Class II antigens are coded for in a region known as HLA-D. The were initially defined by a phenomenon called the mixed lymphocyte reaction, which occurs when lymphocytes from two individuals, who have differing HLA-D regions, are cultured together in vitro. T lymphocytes from one individual recognize ("see") foreign class II antigens on the cells of the other individual and undergo proliferation. If there is genetic identity at the HLA-D region, proliferation does not occur. Recent studies have identified three subregions, DP, DQ, and DR, within the originally defined HLA-D region. Certain class II antigens cannot be detected by the mixed lymphocyte reaction and are designated HLA-D antigens, to distinguish them from the serologically defined D-region antigens, such as HLA -DR and HLA-DQ. However, unlike the case with DP, DQ, and DR loci, there is no single definable special HLA-D locus within the HLA-D region. It is believed that the responding T cells in the mixed lymphocyte reaction are detecting an array of HLA-DP, HLA-DQ, and/or HLA-DR antigens, some of which are not serologically detectable. Class II antigens differ from class I antigens in several respects.

They contain two chains, covalently linked (a - and b - with molecular weights of 34.000 daltons and 29.000 daltons, respectively). Both are highly polymorphic and resemble class I a - chains, also they have constant areas.

Their tissue expression is more limited than in class I antigens. They are normal constituents of APC’s, B-cell’s and activated T-cell’s plasma membrane. But, the phenomenon of induced of abberant expression of class II molecules on somatic cells occurs very frequently, under pathologic conditions and in response to various antigenic challenges. Abnormal expression of class II MHC glycoproteins on somatic cells is responcible for many cases of autoimmune disorders (for instance, autoimmune thyroid diseases), because a cell, expressing MHC II may start to present its own antigens to lymphocytes.

Normally these MHC-proteins are obligatory participants in recognition of the antigens, exocellular for APC. Exocellular does not necessarily mean that these antigens are alien. It means simply that they were engulfed or trapped by APC from around, by means of endocytosis. These antigens have a traffic within APC, which is somewhat different from that of intracellularly produced ones.

Class II molecules are also synthetized within RER, then go into Golgi complex, but ABC-proteins do not bind them. Owing to this, class II MHCmolecules are not able to attach antigenic fragments of endocellular origin. Instead of this, they temporarily connect with the invariant peptide Ii , coded for non-MHC genes. Ii-peptide is a kind of provisional bung, utilized for separation of intracellular class I and class II migration routes. Clas II molecules go into some endocytotic vesicles ( during inflammation and antigenic challenge - quite often, into phagolysosomes of mononuclear phagocytes). There they meet the fragments of engulfed and APC-processed exocellular (not always alien) antigens. Sequence fragment of an antigen ousts the Ii-peptide out of the complex, and new complex of antigen and class II protein goes into plasma membrane. These complex is a subject of interaction (according traditional vocabulary - “double recognition”) with CD4-positive T-regulators (helpers/inducers). The whole chain of events, described above is known.

So, individual amino-acide composition of class II and class I proteins may determine the character and the position of presented antigen fragments, as well as more or less precise manner and results of distinguishing between self and non-self sequences. It is attractive to propose, that the probability of mistakes in recognition (and, hence, the risk of autoimmune disorders, provoked by concrete antigenic challenge in different persons, as well as individual strength of immune response against a given germ) will depend on inherited individual’s MHC haplotype. Epidemiology and medical genetics both have obtained the data, supporting this hypothesis. This opic is discussed later as “MHC and diseases association”.

Both class I and class II MHC molecules, together with TcR, Fc-portions of Igs, secretory fragment of IgA, CD 2,3,4,8 – receptors and some other recognizing proteins belong to superfamily of Ig-genes and their conficuration is close to typical that of antibody molecule. They were originated from common evolutionary progenitor gene and bear some typical features, like multiple polypeptide chains and disulfide briges.

The “class III MHC molecules” are non-homologous to the members of this superfamily. This is traditional name for some humoral effectors of immune system, encoded in 6th chromosome between the genes of MHC I and II.

These are the genes code for complement system components: properdin, C2, C4, and Bf. Besides, here one can found the coding gene for important cytokines - TNF-a and TNF-b . They do not act as histocompatibility (transplantation) antigens, but also are polymorphic. For example, properdin has 4 alternative alleles. The degree (character) of class III antigens’ expression also contribute into immunological individuality of a person. It has been proven, that low expression of several complement factors makes a person predisposed to immune complex reactions and risky for different associated syndromes (vasculitis, glomerulonephritis, arthritis, uveitis). In patients with systemic lupus erythematosus and their close relatives quite frequently occurs heterozygous carriership of null-alleles for C2, C4 and C4B. In absense or in deficiency of these complement fragments, their opsonising function for immune-complex phagocytosis is deranged. Due to this, the clearance of immune complexes, normally produced in everyone after any humoral immune response and engufed by phagocytes, is considerably delayed in persons with complement factors’ deficit. Not extinguished properly by phagocytes, immune complexes get the chance to sediment over vascular wall or cumulate within tissues – and trigger thrombosis and inflammation, damaging self organs (Fig. ).

As already mentioned, a feature shared by class I and class II genes is the high degree of polymorphism. So, each of the several alleles at these loci is designated by a number, such as HLA-A1, HLA-B5, and so forth. (Those alleles that have not been fully characterized are identified by a W (workshop) - e.g., HLA-BW4.) All class I determinants evoke the formation of humoral antibodies in genetically nonindentical individuals. This makes it possible to type these antigens by conventional serologic techniques such as antibody-and complement-mediated lysis. Sera of multiparous women (who are immunized by the paternal antigens of the fetus) have traditionally served as a useful source of such anti-HLA antibodies. The presense of moderate maternal immune response against paternal HLA-I antigens of fetus is essential for appropriate formation of placenta in humans. Hence, placentogenesis looks like a kind of highly specialized productive inflammation induced, by physiological immune conflict between mother and fetus, interpreted by maternal immune system as a kind of tolerable parasite. If parents are close or near-identical in HLA haplotype, placenta formation does not proceed intensively and the resulting placenta is less functional. This phenomenon is the immunological component for the explanation of well-known fact: decreased fetus survival and greater incidense of miscarriage in inbriding. Moreover, the necessity of alien paternal components in fetal haplotype for placenta formation makes the true parthenogenesis in higher mammals impossible.

Now monoclonal anti-HLA antibodies, obtained by immunizing rodents with human cells, are available for diagnostic purposes.

Practical Significance Of MHC Complex

Medical importance of MHC-system is triple. It is related to graft transplantation, to problem of allergy and autoimmunity and to association with certaine immunopathologic and non-immunopathologic diseases.

As stated earlier, HLA antigens were discovered in the course of transplantation studies, and they continue to present main obstacle to the success of clinical organ transplantation. HLA antigens of the graft evoke both humoral and cell-mediated responses, which lead eventually to graft destruction. Because the severity of the rejection reaction is related to the degree of HLA disparity between the donor and recipient, HLA typing is of immense clinical significance in the selection of the donor-recipient combinations.

Class I and II HLA antigens play important but distinct roles in the induction and expression of cellular as well as humoral immunity. Class antigens are involved primarilly in regulating the function of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, which are believed to be important in resistance against virus infections. It can be demonstrated experimentally that cytotoxic T cells specifically sensitized against a virus can recognize and lyse only those virus-infected cells that bear self class I molecules. The basis of this phenomenon of HLA restriction seems to be that class I HLA antigens provide specific sites that are modified by virus infection and constitute the target antigen for the T cell receptors of the cytotoxic (CD8+) T cells. Because virus-infected cells can be recognized only in the context of class I MHC antigens, it makes "good sense" to have widespread expression of class I HLA antigens.

Class II antigens are particularly important in cell-to-cell interactions, especially in the activation of CD4+ T cells. In contrast to CD8+ killer T cells that recognize antigens in association with class I MHC antigens, CD4+ helper T cells recognize antigens only in the context of class II antigens on the surface of macrophages or other antigen-presenting cells. The role of class II antigens in the induction of T helper cells may also have an important bearing on the genetic regulation of the immune response. It is well known that the magnitude of immune responses is controlled by the genes that map within the HLA-D region, previously calld Ir-genes and now proven to be class II. How class II antigens influence the magnitude of the immune response is not fully understood. It is conceivable that certain class II allels may code for cell-surface (class II) molecules that form highly immunogenetic complexes with a given antigen. This in turn would translate into a vigorous activation of T-helper cells and a strong immune response. The consequence of inheriting such a class II gene would depend upon the nature of the antigen and the type of immune response generated. For example, if the antigen were ragweed pollen and the response were production of IgE antibody, the individual would be genetically prone to attcks of sneezing. on the other hand, good responsiveness to influenza virus may well mean a sniffle-free winter. Anyway, if doctor observes the cases of unusual strength of immune response, causing autoimmune disease or allergy, as well as cases of unique immunological weakness, responsible for relinquishment of some individuals against concrete germ strains, - it is necessary to stress that these phenomena may result from individual MHC haplotype specifics.

A variety of diseases have been found in association with certain HLA types (H. Matej, J. Batchelor, 1984). The best known is the association between ankylosing spondylites and HLA-B27; individuals who possess HLA-B27 antigen have a 87-fold greater chance (relative risk) of developing this disease than those who are negative for HLA-B27, and for Reiter’s syndrome relative risk factor in B27 carriers is 37! The diseases that show association with HLA can be broadly grouped into the following categories: (1) immunopathologic inflammatory deseases, including ankylosing spondylitis and several postinfectious arthropathies, all associated with HLA-B27; (2) organospecific autoimmune diseases, like autoimmune endocrinopathies, associated with alleles at the DR locus; (3) non-immunological monogenic inherited errors of metabolism. and (4) complement deficiency syndromes, causing immune-complex disorders.

Concerning 1) and 2) categories, the examples of clinically significant association of certain HLA-alleles and some diseases’ high risk are numerous and striking. HLA D3 and D4 genes are present in as few as 4% of Caucasians, but among white patients with insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (IDDM), which is organ-specific cytotoxic autoimmune disease, D3 either D4 (or both) are encounted in 90% of cases! Mutual presense of these two antigens makes the risk of IDDM 14,3 greater. There is an opinion, that D3 predispose to autoallergy due to decrease of lymphocyte suppression function in its holders. D4 is blamed for decreasing the resistanse against pancreatotropic viruses ( Coxsackie B4, ECHO, german measles and mumps). However, alternative hypothesis stresses that increased risk of IDDM in DR3-4 carriers is related not to D3 and D4 themselves, but to alleles linked with them and belonging to DQ-group. The presence in 53rd position of DQ-protein’s b - chain any other amino-acid, except aspartate, elevates the risk of IDDM. DQ3-allele is linked with higher risk of celiakia.

The allele DR3 is very often expressed in association with different autoimmune disorders, manifested with antibodies towards cellular receptors and/or in autoimmune endicrinopathies. Not infrequently, DR3 is combined with B8 and A2 class I antigens. The risk of Addison’s disease, which is commonly caused by chronic autoimmune adrenalitis, is 7 times greater amongst the bearers of DR3-antigen. D. Fostman et al (1991) proposed that the increased risk of autoimmune endocrine diseases, including IDDM and disorders of thyroid and adrenal glands, can be related to some inherited defect of intracellular carrier ABC-protein, taking part in antigen presentation by MHC-class I molecules.

B27 gene is associated with increased risk of disorders manifested in non-organospecific autoallergy (autoimmune diseases of connective tissue).

There is a clear linkage between DR3 and DR5 genes and autoimmune thyropathies, including Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (relative risk factor of about 3,5). Last gene is also related to increased risk of autoimmune aplastic anemia, namely the form, provoked in it’s carriers by parvovirus B19 infection. DR2 carriers are highly risky for Goodpasture’s syndrome (relative risk quotient 15,9) and multiple sclerosis (4,1).

DR4 gene is associated with higher risk of myasthenia gravis (autoimmune block of N-cholinergic receptors) and also with rheumatoid arthritis. Juvenile form of last disease (Still’s syndrome)is related to DR5-antigen.

There is a linkage between gene B8 and atopic diseases, gene B5 – and Behcet’s disease, as well as duodenal peptic ulcer. It has been proven, that different persons vary in intensity of anti-parasitic immunity depending on their MHC-haplotype.

Speaking about 3) category, we mean the inborn diseases caused by defective genes, occasionally located close to some of MHC-alleles, but, probably irrelevant to immunopathologic mechanisms. A typical example is the gene of primary hemochromatosis, wide spread in european populations, especially among French. It is located also in 6th chromosome, in its short arm, close to MHC class I genes and display obvious linkage disequilibrium with A3 (and, to lesser extent also with B7 , B14). Similar non-immunologic linkage exists between B47 and congenital adrenal cortex hyperplasia (caused by 21-steroid-hydroxilase deficiency and especially common among Eskimos). There is a relation between B35 – and subacute thyroiditis, B47 – and psoriasis. The immunopathologic nature of last two diseases is strongly suspeed, but not yet proven.

As a kind of resume for the discussion of this hot problem, we mast conclude, that the mechanisms underlying associations between MHC and disease are different and only partially understood at present. The following two mechanisms have been proposed.

Involvement of immune response genes. It was mentioned previously that HLA class II genes can regulate immune responsiveness. Accordingly, an association between certain autoimmune diseases and HLA-DR antigens may result from an exaggerated immune response to autoantigens.

Direct participation of HLA macromolecules in disease. There are two possible mechanisms by which HLA molecules may participate directly in disease. First, pathogens may share a cross-reacting antigen with HLA and be protected from an immune response by the host's tolerance for self-HLA antigens ("molecular mimicry"). This in effect would provide the pathogen an "immunologic permit" to invade and destroy tissues at will.

Alternatively, a strong immune response may be mounted against the invading pathogen, but because of the sharing of determinants between the microbe and the HLA antigen, immunologically mediated damage may be spread over normal tissues.

Second, certain HLA molecules may provide receptors for viruses, and this may either facilitate virus-cell interaction or provide a target for host immune cells to destroy virus-infected cells. In the former case, the presence of a given HLA type would favor virus-induced disease, whereas in the latter case it would favor removal of virus-infected cells.

A student, of course, understands that certain MHC haplotypes do not automatically make their host sick and are of no relation with the attempts to separate the mankind on sorts or strata.

For realization of probability, created by MHC specifics, an individual needs to undergo the action of some trigger cause, as a rule – some kind of exogenous antigenic provocation. This challenge is not always infectious. The presense of some MHC-proteins is a prerequisite of certain drug-induced allergic and autoimmune reactions. For example, hydralazine triggers lupus-like syndrome in carriers of DR4, the haplotypes DR3 and DR5 predispose to systemic sclerosis after the contact with vinyl-chloride, haplotypes A1, B8, Cw7 and DR3 are common for patients treated with colloid gold preparations and suffering from jatrogenic adverse nephropathy. The relations of this kind are of utmost interest for clinical pharmacology.

Anyway, association of MHC-genes and diseases makes concrete the classic concept of constitutio corporis, known from ancient times, and gives definite molecular critheria to the relationship between human constitution and diseases, empirically noticed long ago.

Conclusion

Having reviewed the fundamentals of BASIC IMMUNOLOGY, we can now turn to general disorders of the immune system and some specific immunologic diseases. These will be discussed under four broad headings: (1) hypersensitivity reactions, which form the mechanisms of immunologic injury in a variety of diseases, sphere of ALLERGOLOGY ; (2) autoimmune diseases, which are caused by immune reactions against self, sphere of AUTOIMMUNITY; and (3) immunologic deficiency syndromes, which result from relatively distinct or complex, genetically determined or acquired defects in some coomponents of the normal immune response, discussed in the section of immunodeficiencies.

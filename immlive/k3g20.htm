TYPE III HYPERSENSITIVITY (IMMUNE COMPLEX–MEDIATED ALLERGY): IMMUNE COMPLEXES' CLEARANCE

Type III hypersensitivity reaction is induced by antigen-antibody complexes that produce inflammation as a result of their capacity to activate a variety of serum mediators, principally the complement system, and to cause accumulation and activation of polymorphonuclear leukocytes.

The toxic reaction is initiated when antigen combines with antibody, whether within the circulation (circulating immune complexes) or at extravascular sites where antigen may have been deposited (in situ immune complexes).

Immune complexes are formed in virtually all individuals, and their production accompanies every humoral immune response. But only some immune complexes in separate individuals, predisposed to this category of hypersensitivity, cause immune complex disorders. The crucial role is played by fate of the of immune complexes in organism.

Immune complex reactions occur if normal clearance of immune complexes is deranged or relatively insufficient. In this situation immune complexes, instead of engulfment by phagocytes and subsequent inactivation, may be deposited on vascular walls or within tissues. In such a location they can activate inflammation, thrombosis and fibrin production mechanisms – in different sites through the body. Both exogenous and endogenous antigens may cause the formation of complexes, so not only chronic infections (malaria, bronchopulmonary mycoses), but also chronic autoimmune disorders (systemic lupus erythematosus). According modern concept, the predisposition to immune complex (IC) mediated diseases is related to the group of hereditary defects in physiological system of IC clearance. Overload of phagocytes with IC may exhaust this channel of filtering out and also facilitates IC pathology.

In Primates the leading part in this function belongs to RBC, complement and macrophages. Normally the IC are trapped by erythrocytes via С3в fragment of complement and CR1 receptor of RBC. This prevents IC from contact with vascular wall, because major part of RBC follows the axial laminar blood flow. In splenic and hepatic sinusoids, in turbulent blood flow, RBC coated with IC, are engulfed by macrophages via Fc-fragments and Fc-receptors. Some part of free immune complexes, lost by carriers as a result of complement factor I action, is engulfed by macrophages directly, without the assistance of RBC. IgA activate the complement via alternative pathway, which creates the immune complexes with presence of C3b fragments in their content. IgG activate classic pathway of complement, which requires non-covalent reversible fixation of C1q fragments to Сg 2 -domain of IgG Fc-portion between amino-acid residues N 282 and N 292. After antigen binding the angular distance between Fab-portions of forked Ig G molecule increases. Fc – portion goes out of the flat, where F(ab)2-fork is located. This steric change facilitates the temporary fixation of C1q fragment. C1q has 2.5 times greater size than IgG does. Due to this, fixation of this complement fragment hides a part of Fc-portion. The C1q acting as a shield creates an obstacle for macrophagal trapping of IC. This stereometric phenomenon, known as “interference og Ig effector functions” was described by A. Ja. Kuhlberg (1986). In course of subsequent complement activation steps, C3b fragment non-covalently joins the IC (binding the Fd-portion of Fab-fragment). This event causes the steric changes of the whole IC, facilitating the loss of C1q and macrophagal elimination of IC. The joining of C3b makes the IC more soluble, promoting the loss of C4b. Consequently, IgG-containing IC have C3b and C4b. IgM-containing ones have not only these complement fragments, but also C1q (A.Kuhlberg, 1986,1991; H. Takahashi, 1980). All this witness for great possible role of acquired and inborn defects in this IC clearance system for the pathogenesis of IC-mediated diseases. The concept of IC clearance derangement as main link of IC pathology is confirmed by many clinical observations.

  • IC syndromes are common in patients with hereditary C3 deficiency.
  • Low serum complement levels (hypocomplementemia) is observed in active phase of any IC disease.
  • The syndromes with redundant activation of alternative complement pathway (sepsis, toxic shock, burn disease) are known as risky for IC disorders.
  • Considerable part of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) patients (not less than 6%) have inborn defects of complement fragments and impaired removal of IC by macrophages.
  • The genetic defects of immunoglobulin Fc-portion and/or macrophagal Fc-receptor are discovered by T. Frank et al. (1983) in many SLE patients and their closest relatives.
  • The autoantibodies towards alternative complement pathway convertase – C3bBb-fragment cause in some patients C3b deficit and membranoproliferative IC glomerulonephritis of II type (sometimes called “dense deposit disease” due to deposition of dense material in glomerular basal membrane). C3b presents in irregular granular-linear deposits of IC along basal membrane and within characteristic mesangial rings. These autoantibodies are main players of the whole pathogenesis, because they activate and stabilize convertase of alternative pathway, promoting progressive loss of C3 and decrease of IC clearance rates. They have the special name: nephritic (nephritogenic) factor (S.R. Nussinov et al. 1985).
  • In most common glomerular disease worldwide – Ig A nephropathy (Berger’s disease), as well as in almost 30% of Henoch-Schonlein purpura patients, there are excessive production of IgA by mucosal immune system, decreased secretion of these antibodies and diminished rate of their liver clearance. As a result, Ig A-containing IC, activating alternative pathway of complement, are deposited in glomeruli and hypocomplementemia occurs.

In all these disorders the time of IC circulation in blood is shortened due to their rapid vascular wall and tissue fixation.

Anyway, the lack of appropriate IC removal by phagocytes may result from the difference in rate of their production and rate of their elimination. This can be caused by the following mechanisms:

  • Absence or defect of complement and/or Fc-receptors on blood cells and phagocytes
  • Deficit or inactivation of C3b complement fragment, including consumption of this fragment in processes, exhausting complement system (toxic shock, sepsis, burns, pseudoallergic reactions etc.)
  • Formation of immune complexes of “complex sandwich type”, containing antiisotypic autoantibodies (rheumatoid factors), autoantibodies towards complement factors (immunoconglutinin, nephritic factor), anti-idiotypic autoantibodies or Ig-binding proteins of Cocci (staphylococcal protein A and streptococcal protein M). In last case the main role belongs to close homology between these proteins and antibody Fc fragments, as well as creation of antiidiotypic internal immunological images of coccal proteins by immune system (P. Christensen, A. Schroder, 1990).
  • The size of IC is of some significance for the rate of their removal and for their final destiny. A common sense-born idea, that “big things” sediment easier and damage worse, than “small ones” is applicable to snow, hail, meteorites and bricks falling down one’s head. But it is absolutely vise versa for IC! Very large complexes, formed in great Ig excess are removed by phagocytes more rapidly and can cause no harm. It is true, for example, for IgM-containing IC. But, small IC produced in slight excess of an antigen, circulate longer, and phagocytes engulf them more readily. So, they are more pathogenic (e.g. those with Ig A which do not activate classic pathway and, hence, not trapped by RBC). In Ig A dependent IC syndromes exacerbation may be provoked by acute respiratory or gut infection, because these situations are known for rapid increase in Ig A production.

It should be pointed out at the outset that the mere formation of antigen - antibody complexes in the circulation does not imply the presence of disease. IC serum level is determined by means of assay with their fixation to Raji cells. IC can also be topically located in tissues by means of immunomorphological analysis (light fluorescent microscopy of Ig by Coombs’ test and/or electron microscopic location of IC deposits. The interpretation of high circulating IC level as risky and low circulating IC level – as safe is first offering itself, but for sure it is wrong and insufficient. The IC cause disease only if they no longer circulate. The potential pathogenicity of circulating IC depends greatly on their qualitative characteristics and on capacity of removing cells. In addition to size and class of Ig, several other factors, such as charge of the immune complexes (anionic versus cationic), valency of the antigen, avidity of the antibody, the affinity of the antigen to various tissue components, the three-dimensional (lattice) structure of the complexes, and hemodynamic factors, influence their tissue deposition. F. Hay (1994) believes, that IC deposition is promoted, first of all, by immune responses with production of abnormal low affinity antibodies and antiidiotypic autoantibodies in lack of appropriate suppressive mechanisms. Taking all these factors into account, perhaps, best approach to circulating IC level test is to control it several times, in dynamics, relying upon decrease of circulating IC level as indication for extensive IC tissue deposition. Also, it is advisable to perform parallel check for hypocomplementemia.

Preface

Sacrilegiously we may compare the living organism to a programmed device because its adaptation is based on programmed responses. The repertoire of these responses (sometimes designated as reactivity) includes the entire diversity of individual genetic programs plus the epigenetic individual experience, superimposed over the genetic background and developed within genetically determined limits. Reactivity includes also some individual preference or tendency to select certain patterns of response from this available spectrum. We may say that reactivity of an organism is not only a library, but also a librarian. In a wide variety of situations, pathogenic signal starts some protective program, which is more or less universal and does not depend (or depends to a minor extent) on the character of the challenge. The examples of these non-specific defensive responses are thrombosis, inflammation, hyperemia etc. At the same time, even in the most primitive unicellular prokaryotic organisms one may observe the specific reactions, matched to the character of triggering stimulus. The most ancient example is, probably, an adaptive induction of enzyme biosynthesis, provoked by complementary specific substrates.

During the evolution living organisms developed a special system capable of creating and storing addressed protective programs individually picked up in relation to concrete pathogens. This system is based on inducible protein biosynthesis, and, if necessary, it has the property to unzip these programs from archive and to carry them out in the amplified (boosted) form.

This system has sensory functions as regards the molecular nature of self and alien biopolymers; it possesses the memory of the previous signals and learning capability.

 

By virtue of these features the repeated answers to the same pathogen differ from the initial one. The assemblage mentioned is the immune system.

 

 

Antigens

Living organism perseveres in the unfriendly environment in spite of mechanical, physical, chemical and biological pathogenic factors (pathogens) trying to harm it from without and a lot of unfavorable tendencies injuring it from within. The only way to survive and flourish is to permanently strain all its systems to achieve a balance between all kinds of damaging influences and the efforts of the organism to compensate thus maintaining homeostasis. The role of the immune system in achieving this goal is unrivaled, because the immune system sustains the homeostasis on the sub-cellular level being uniquely equipped with a mechanism to produce a myriad of special instruments, every of them specifically complimentary to one of a billion of various biopolymers molecule types. These molecules are called antigens and the specific instruments - antigen-specific lymphocyte receptors.

A substance is antigenic provided it has three-dimensional structure complicated enough for complementary key-lock principle interactions. In other words, it must be able to recognize and to be recognizable. Paul Ehrlich has formulated this principle in proverbial form: “ Corpora non agunt nisi fixata.” Antigenicity requires some degree of structural complexity and spatial rigidity. Even molecules with high molecular weight can be non-antigenic, having flat structure (cholesterol). The gelatin is high molecular weight protein, although devoid of tertiary structure and, as a result – non-antigenic (not able to form “key-hole”) (W. Boyd, 1969).

To coin a definition the antigens are biopolymeric molecules capable of inciting immune response in the organism by specifically binding to the lymphocyte antigen-specific receptors.

The antigens are a wide variety of natural and biosynthetic molecules sized between 1 and 10 kD (proteins, polysaccharides, complex esters and heterocyclic substances, nucleic acids, polynucleotides and their complexes, including lipid-containing ones).

The definition of antigen has been changed greatly through last 50 years. Initially it was regarded as simple synonym of “pathogen”, later immunologists re-interpreted this term as direct complementary cast of antibody, and finally it came to the definition mentioned above. Hence, antigens are polymeric molecules able to trigger specific immune response when introduced into organism. Interestingly, only polar molecules are highly immunogenic.

At the same time, highly spread popular interpretation of the word “antigen” as “alien protein” must be rejected from the very beginning of present discussion because of its anecdotal and outdated character. Moreover, many low molecular weight substances, like iodine, may generate unique three-dimensional complexes with biopolymeric carriers and provoke immune responses. They are called haptens.

An antigen is regarded by immunochemistry as individual one only if its components are adjoined by covalent chemical bounds. From this standpoint, cells, bacteria and multimolecular complexes possess manifold antigenic specificity. The minimal structural unit of an antigen, recognizable for antibodies and lymphocyte antigenic receptors is known as epitope or immunodominant region.

As a rule, epitopes are located in most mobile and configurable parts of quite rigid antigen structure. Each clone of lymphocytes is genetically programmed to recognize only one epitope, by means of an antigen- specific receptor on its surface, and hence these cells display exquisite antigen specificity. It is believed that billions of different epitopes may exist, consequently the equivalent number of lymphoid cell clones with their unique receptors is required for the recognition of this plenty.

The immune response requires certain density of epitopes: it disappears if their compactness is too high or too low. Single epitopes are non-immunogenic because for immune response the recognizing molecules must be cross-linked. Due to this overwhelming majority of immune responses needs cooperation of several specialized cells ( B-lymphocytes, T-lymphocytes, antigen-presenting cells ).

Some antigens include panels of numerous identical epitopes. Among them bacterial lipopolysaccharides, polymeric protein of Flagellates–flagellin, dextran, levan and other bacterial capsule polysaccharides, ficoll and polypeptides composed of D-aminoacids. They are hardly digestible for antigen-presenting cells, but able to cross-link the recognizing lymphocyte receptors directly. This group of antigens provokes polyclonal B-cell activation and immunoglobulin production without T-cell cooperation. Many individual antigens have several or even numerous different epitopes and elicit immune response of various cellular clones, quite often simultaneously in different forms (both antibody secretion and clonal expansion of cellular effectors).

All antigens may be formally classified into exogenous and endogenous depending on their origin. This distinction is relative for it depends on whether you look at the antigens from within this or that organism. Therefore the endogenous and exogenous antigens do not differ in shape, nature, size, smell or taste. Even the attitude of the immune system almost does not depend on whether the antigen be endogenous or exogenous, e.g., exogenous antigens of transfused donor red blood cells are tolerated, whereas the endogenous antigens arising on the recipients' own olden erythrocytes incite a cold-blooded murder of these battered veterans by the immune system. To be more exact, antigen-presenting cell process differently the antigens, coming from within the cell itself and those engulfed from without. But, it does not matter if the endocellular antigen is self or, for example, viral and if extracellular one is bacterial, or belongs to the organism's own senescent cell.

Of much greater significance for immune system is a classification categorizing all antigens into autoantigens (self) and heteroantigens (non-self). Endoantigens are not necessarily autoantigens. The autoantigens are the biopolymers characteristic of youthful normal unimpaired cells; although these molecules are superabundant, they normally produce no other response but immunologic tolerance. The markers of senescence, expressed on aged cells and pathologic neoantigens, induced by viral infection, trauma, and oncogenesis - do not belong to autoantigens. All these antigens are included in modified-self group and treated by immune system as alien ones. The heteroantigens are, on the other hand, the molecules that can not be found in youthful normal unimpaired cells, on the contrary, they appear in sick, olden, injured or tumor cells, as well as alien ones, or invade the organism from without. These heteroantigens are not tolerated normally, but rather produce full-fledged immune response. Without a doubt, the heteroantigens may be of exogenous as well as endogenous origin (neoantigens and modified-self ones). On the other hand there are both endogenous autoantigens (which is only natural), and exogenous (cross-reactive) ones.

It takes all sorts of antigens to make the immune system work properly for they are the major objet it manipulates.

In fact, antigen is not only material molecule, but also information unit or signal.

As any kind of new information, antigens need presentation for broad recognition and acknowledgement.

According to the classic concept, antigens display the epitopes of different types. Conformation (discontinuous) determinants represent the forms created by tertiary structure of biopolymers. For example, in protein antigens it may be represented by amino acid residues located far from each other in primary polypeptide chain, but put together in spatial three-dimensional globes of fibrils. As a rule, they protrude out of the whole antigen molecule. Sequential (continuous) determinants are all but simple and short linear sequences produced by primary structure, for example short peptides of  8-9 to 20-28 amino acids in protein molecule.

In course of immune response for extracellular antigens, during antigen processing after phagocytosis, conformation epitopes are lost, but sequential are exposed and presented by antigen-presenting cells on their surface, as a rind of panel. In course of immune response for intracellular antigens, sequential determinants are created in translation and presented by antigen-presenting cells before complete set of conformation determinants is acquired in post-translation modifications.

 

The Elements of Immune System

The immune system (immunitas in Latin means freedom (from disease)) is a source of complex and highly sophisticated defense strategies that is often subdivided into two major categories, innate and adaptive, each endowed with a humoral and cellular arm. The immune system consists of the numerous solid and dispersed circulating elements, with total mass in adult person of approximately 1,5 kg (Fig.1). The central organs of immunogenesis are those, where immature lymphocytes develop and are exposed to primary clonal selection.

In humans these are bone marrow and thymus. The peripheral organs, where mature lymphocytes live, undergo antigen-dependant clonal expansion and carry out the immune responses, include, besides spleen and lymph nodes, also Waldeier-Pirogov lymphoepithelial pharyngeal ring and diffuse non-encapsulated lymphoid elements of gastrointestinal, bronchial and urogenital mucosa.

The bone marrow carries out both functions of central and peripheral lymphoid organ, being a place of clonal differentiation as well as clonal expansion. The blood is also part of immune system, as the elements of this system, both specific, and non- specific, have the ability to circulate, which is essential for their function. It concerns to lymphoid cells, immunoglobulins (Ig), complement proteins and another effectors of the immune response. For this reason, local sensitization can proceed in system sensitivity.

Interaction of specific cellular elements (lymphocytes) and their products (antibodies and cytokines) provide the optimum performance of immune system. They interact with each other, and also with non-lymphoid elements. Majors of the last ones are antigen presenting cells (APC).

Immune system becomes more and more complicated in a course of evolution. The recognition of alien entities is traced in Spongia, and alien rejection is observed since Hydrae. In Anthoza immunologic memory and cytotoxicity are described. Since lowest Vertebrates there is an interaction of APC and effector cells of immunity (E.L. Cooper, 1979). Some Annelid worms have leukocytes, capable to antigen-induced proliferation. The precise division on Т- and B- lymphocytes is revealed in Fishes. The antibodies such as IgM occur in Cyclostomata. The Amphibians have IgG, and the Bird - also IgA. The fishes have thymus and spleen, non-caudate Amphibians form lymph nodes and bone marrow, and Birds - Fabricius bursa. Finally, only and exclusively supreme placentate Mammals produce IgE (М. J. Manning, 1979).

The innate resistance system may be viewed as the first line of defense. Its cellular components, are ideally suited for this role because their actions do not depend on prior sensitization or "priming" by the antigens present on their target cells (e.g., microbes, tumor cells) and furthermore they do not possess the fine antigen specificity that is characteristic of the adaptive (or specific) immune responses. Thus, for example, a given macrophage can whet its appetite by ingesting a wide variety of antigens. Complement system is the major humoral component of innate immunity. Innate immunity causes so called pre-immune response or acute phase response after immediately invasion of any infect or non-infectious antigen. Innate non-specific mechanisms are embedded into the building of specific immune response as very essential bricks or even milestones. Due to opportunistic nature of evolution (R. Gullielmin, 1991), archetypes or old defensive programs, once discovered by evolution, are not abandoned after appearance of newer ones, but updated and used for the purposes established by new programs. The phagocytosis appeared in unicellular organisms, long before immune system rise, serves for the aims of antigen processing and presentation. Complement and other ancient effectors of inflammation are enhanced and specifically targeted by younger warrior of evolution - immune response. The principal cellular components of the adaptive immune responses are T and B-lymphocytes.

As a rule, primary immune response for an antigen modifies the immune system. Repeated penetration of the same antigen results in boosted secondary immune response. This phenomenon traditionally is called by figurative name “positive immunologic memory”. The memory is antigen-specific, unlike so-called anamnestic response, last meaning just non-specific increase in immunogenesis by any antigen against different ones due to universal production of immunostimulant cytokines. Primary non-boosted immune response gives noticeable titers of circulating antibodies 1-2 weeks after first contact with an antigen. Maximal antibody production is achieved 1-2 months later, than their concentration declines. IgM are initially predominant in primary response, with later rise in Ig G titers. If the antigen is already known to organism, repeated exposure produce more rapid and strong response, with noticeable titer of specific antibodies achieved as early as in few days, with much higher maximal level, remaining stable for many months. Ig G predominates from the very beginning of secondary immune response. In some cases first contact with an antigen changes the immune system in such a way, that repeated stimulation causes no response or produces decreases response. This is the situation sometimes mentioned as negative immunologic memory or acquired immunologic tolerance.

The main criteria of actual immunologic reactions are specificity and immunologic memory. A cell recognizes glucose or aspirin by means of specific target molecules (receptors), but this does not cause immunologic memory for such substrates, consequently similar reactions are not considered to be immunologic.

The term “memory cell” is very popular in immunologic literature, but it is necessary to remind that it is just allegory. There are no cells which “remember only doing nothing else”. The fable word “memory” is used just to stress the ability for enhanced boosted immune response – and nothing more exotic. It means that all T- and B-lymphocyte appeared after primary immune response as a result of clonal selection and antigen-induced clonal expansion process are real memory cells.

They are able to respond in enhanced manner after contact with specific antigen, and their B-subset starts antibody production immediately with IgG, unlike their ancestors, beginning with IgM.

The majority of antigens are not able to cross-link the lymphocyte receptors. Due to this, regulatory T-cells can respond only if an antigen is presented by APC, as a panel of sequential determinants, in association with the glycoproteins of major histocompatibility complex (MHC).

 

B-cells do recognize conformation epitopes of the intact antigen and, principally, are capable to respond autonomously for the epitopes, complementary to their surface immunoglobulin receptors. But they need T cell help for full-fledged complete immune response. Moreover, many antigens in rough natural form induce tolerance of B-cells on direct contact.

The general features of the immune system can be discussed as follows.

During evolution, humans have developed the ability to recognize myriad of foreign antigens and hence a normal individual must possess antigen-specific cells that can react with a staggering number of antigens. Because the human body is not merely a bad of lymphocytes (as many immunologists believe!), it follows that in unprimed individuals the number of lymphocytes that can react to any given specific antigen must be extremely small. However, when challenged with an antigen, say in the form of old, sick, damaged or tumor cells, microbial infection, antigen-specific lymphocytes divide and expand to effectively handle the unwelcome inmate. Cellular components of immune system, both specific, and not specific, can proliferate. The multiplication of lymphocytes occurs not only in the central immune organs: the secondary duplication of stimulated cells takes place also peripherally. This feature allows considerable increasing of the immune reactions, but also requires controlling and braking mechanisms for prevention of the accidental or excessive answers. All examined immunologic reactions are under the control and represent intermingling balanced network of stimulatory and suppressing signals.

Such clonal expansions of T and B lymphocytes is a complex multi-step process involving both differentiation and cell division; therefore, it takes at least a few days, sometimes weeks, before the process is completed. Because of this requirement, specific immune responses are better suited as the second line of defense, which eventually becomes the major protective mechanism.

Although the distinctions between innate and induced immunity and the cellular and humoral effector systems are helpful resolving the complexity of the host defense systems, it is extremely important to remember that there are multiple interconnections and "cross talk" among the various components. Recall that the complement cascade, an important nonspecific humoral component, can be triggered directly by an invading organism (the alternate pathway) or by antibodies formed in the course of a specific immune response. Complement, in turn, plays an important role in modulating the function of neutrophils and macrophages, as detailed earlier. Macrophages are not only important effector cells in the expression of innate immunity, but also, as antigen-presenting cells, they play an important role in triggering specific immune responses mediated by B and T lymphocytes. The latter, as is well known, mediate function of antibody-producing B cells as well as that of macrophages and natural killer (NK) cells. The physiologic significance of these interactions is often evident when a genetic or acquired disease selectively destroys just one component of this finely tuned "orchestra", as is exemplified by the acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS). In this disorder, the destruction of a subset of T cells by the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) produces a cascade of effects that eventually cripple almost the entire host defense apparatus.

Although vital to survival, the immune system is like the proverbial two-edged sword. On the one hand, immunodeficiency states render humans easy prey to infections and possibly tumors; on the other hand, a hyperactive immune system may cause fatal disease, as in the case of an overwhelming allergic reaction to the sting of a bee. In yet another series of derangement, the immune system may lose its normal capacity to distinguish self from non-self, resulting in the emergence of immunity against one's own tissues and cells (autoumminity). In this chapter, we consider diseases caused by too little immunity as well as those resulting from too much immunologic reactivity. But first, we review some recent advances in the understanding of lymphocyte biology, to be followed by a brief description of the histocompatibility genes, because their products are relevant to several immunologically mediated diseases and to the rejection of transplants.

Lymphoid Organs

The discussion of immune defense benefits and costs shall be started with the description of lymphoid organs’ structure and histophysiology.

After birth lymphocytes are, mostly, generated in central lymphoid organs: thymus and bone marrow, which are responsible for the development of their clonal diversity and tolerance (these processes together are called priming), although clonal expansion may occur also in peripheral lymphoid tissue.

The Bone Marrow

The Bone Marrow, which is, in fact, both central and peripheral lymphoid organ shall be discussed first. The progenitors of B lymphocytes develop in humans within yolk sac and fetal liver, after birth this [process concentrates in bone marrow. In postnatal period bone marrow produces both T- and B-cells from common lymphoid progenitor cell. B-cells are produced in this compartment of immune system through all life long. It is much more complex process than simple proliferation. B-cell differentiation suggests active somatic mutation and alternative splicing of the genes, encoding the variants of different immunoglobulin portions. This creates the clonal diversity of B-lymphocytes, which leave bone marrow differentiated enough.

Unlike B-cells, T-lymphocytes precursors, produced within bone marrow and diversified as a set of clones with different structure of surface antigenic receptor, are still not mature and transmigrate into thymus for subsequent differentiation with parallel positive and negative clonal selection or priming.

As a peripheral lymphoid organ, bone marrow contains, along with hematopoetic progenitors, also mature B and T-cells, macrophages and their ancestors. If progenitor cell perish within bone marrow (due to: irradiation, exposure to chemical mutagens, viruses, expansion and competition of malignant atypical cells, autoimmune cytolytic or cytostatic effect) patient may suffer from lymphopenia and immunodeficiency. Constant gene recombination within lymphoid progenitor cells creates relatively high risk of somatic mutation provoking expression of oncogenes. As a result of it, atypical lymphoid blasts may arise, causing lymphogenous leukemia or lymphomas. These types of malignancies, according P. Chandrasoma and C.R. Taylor (1998), totally occupy sixth place in incidence among neoplasias in population of North America. Quite frequently aplastic and neoplastic processes of lymphoid clones are combined within bone marrow of the same patient.

 

 

 

The Thymus

Thymic Gland or Thymus: is an organ of rather complex structure, which was believed to be the “Seat of the Soul and Courage” by Ruphus of Ephesus (100 B.C.) and also by ancient Hindu wisemen. It has retrosternal location and consists of two lobes and many lobules. Each of them contains cortical and medullar substances (M.D. Kendall, 1981). Tightly packed small T-lymphocytes are predominant in thymus cortex. There are also epithelial nurse-cells.

The medullar substance, besides T-cells (more mature and less tightly packed than in the cortex, supplied with CD44 marker), has also the mesh of epithelial cells, attached to stroma and contacting with lymphocytes. The specific dense structures formed by knot of medullar epithelial cells, degenerated in apoptosis, is called Hassall’s corpuscles. Thymic stroma and stellate or D-cells (APC analogues) are derived from 3rd and 4th pharyngeal pouches. These cells present MHC glycoproteins to local maturing thymocytes (lymphoid T-cells), which came from yolk sac, fetal liver and bone marrow and settled within thymus. MHC antigen presentation is necessary for priming or development of MHC restriction.

The intensive mitoses of lymphocytes and programmed death of T-cells both determine the definite set of mature T-clones, designated to leave thymus and settle within peripheral lymphoid organs.

Not all, but few T-cells, enrolled at Thymic School will graduate from it. After complete course of “education” the survived graduates, able to interact with autologious cells of immune system, but tolerant to autoantigens leave thymus via venules. For a long time crucial role of thymus in immunogenesis was not appreciated. As late as in 1963, such an authoritative immunologist as Noble Prize winner (1960), discoverer of acquired immunologic tolerance, P. Medavar still wrote sincerely and mistakenly: “ we shall come to regard the presence of lymphocytes in the thymus as an evolutionary accident of no very great significance”. Yet, in 1961-1962 J.F.A.P. Miller from Australia performed the decisive experiments, allowed him to conclude that: “ during embryogenesis the thymus would produce the originators of immunologically competent cells, many of which would have migrated to other sites at about the time of birth. This would suggest that lymphocytes, leaving the thymus are specially selected cells”. Later (1967-1968) the same author together with J.F. Mitchell have demonstrated that lymphocytes come into thymus from bone marrow and are educated there by means of positive and negative selection mechanisms.

The defects in thymus embryogenesis cause the disorder of T-cell maturing resulting in immunodeficiency.

The examples are complete aplasia of thymus – Di George’s syndrome and thymic dysplasia – Nezeloff’s syndrome.

Thymic disorders alter the process of autoreactive clones negative selection and thus provoke autoimmune diseases.

 

The thymus is subject of age-dependent accidental involution, which probably causes some decrease of immune surveillance and violations of self-tolerance in elderly individuals.

The stress accelerates the rate of thymic involution and triggers the apoptosis in thymocytes.

 

 

Thymus is not only immune, but also endocrine organ (Z. Kemileva, 1984). Several polypeptide hormones are produced by epithelial thymic cells and increted into blood. Thymopoietin (also known as thymosin) facilitates the maturing of T-cells and displays corticoliberin-like activity. Thymulin is another thymic hormone. It is Zn-containing peptide, essential for immunogenesis. That’s why, zinc deficient diet is dangerous for decrease of immunity (J. -F. Bach, 1984). Besides these true hormones, epithelial thymic cells, T-lymphocytes of thymus and migrant macrophages and APC secrete a lot of other cytokines.

Thymic aplasia is characteristic for deficit of T-cell cytokines, especially IL-2, and therefore in these patients both cellular and humoral immune reactions may be deranged.

Hyperplasia of thymus is associated with peculiar change of reactivity – status thymico-lymphaticus commonly listed in classic medical texts among so called diatheses. Thymico-lymphatic status is characteristic for thymus hyperplasia and delay of it’s age-related atrophy. Peripheral lymphoid organs also are somewhat enlarged. This is accompanied by hypoplasia of adrenals and even tendency to hypofunction of these endocrine glands. It is believed that ability to stress response is decreased in these individuals. In accordance with classic concept, there is some relation between status thymico-lymphaticus and increased risk of sudden infant’s death syndrome, although most recent researches did not witness for such correlation.

From the immunological point of view, there is one very essential detail: in thymus hyperplasia B-lymphoid follicles appear within thymus, although normally B-cells are absent in thymic gland. The persons with thymic hyperplasia often display some disorders of self-tolerance. The bright example is high incidence of myasthenia gravis with autoimmunity towards N-cholinergic receptor among the individuals having thymic hyperplasia or thymic epithelial malignant tumor – thymoma.

Thymoma is commonly accompanied by some of the long spectrum of autoimmune disorders (aplastic anemia, systemic lupus erythematosus, and autoimmune endocrinopathies). Thymus is involved also in T-cell lymphoblastic leukemia and in T-lymphomas, which as a rule are metastatic (from bone marrow) and quite rarely originate locally, within thymus.

 

 

Having discussed the main features of the central lymphoid organs, we shall proceed to the peripheral ones.

The peripheral lymphoid organs and clusters of lymphocytes contain mature lymphocytes and accessory APC as well. They are distributed far and wide through the organism. Immunocompetent cells present even behind the blood brain barrier (intrathecal lymphocytes and microglial cells of CNS). However, there exists a strong tendency for lymphoid tissue to occur predominantly at portals of antigenic entry. The relative percentage of T and B-cells varies in peripheral compartments of immune system, depending on location (see table 1 below).

The Spleen

The Spleen works as the lymphoid filter in blood circulation system. This is an important seat of antibody production, generally in response to circulating cellular and bacterial antigens, brought with blood flow. The lymphocytes also came into spleen with blood flow. The spleen consists of the capsule and trabecules or cords, protruding into its parenchyma. Capsule and cords support the red pulp and white pulp. The red pulp, mostly, consists of macrophages and other reticuloendothelial elements, surrounding the venous sinusoids. The sparse plasma cells (B-lymphocytes, producing antibodies) are scattered among them. The white pulp is presented with lymphoid tissue, forming nodules around the arteries and arterioles. The penicillary (in European texts – central) arteries branch from the trabecular arteries and ramify within the lymphoid nodules (in classic texts – Malpighian corpuscles). The periarteriolar muffs or sheaths envelop the central arterioles, diverging from penicillary arteries. These sheaths are stuffed with the alumni of Thymus School – T-lymphocytes. B-lymphocytes settle in another ghetto of an organ – they form primary follicles and, in course of immune responses - also secondary follicles with germinal centers, both types of B-follicles are located laterally from T-dependent zones. The follicles contain the APC for B-cells (dendritic cells).

The spleen is the major arena of gladiatorial combats for destroy of the microbial and self opsonized cells, for example sepsis agents or damaged and senescent blood cells. This process occurs within red pulp. For sure, there is no medical student who is not familiar with the sacramental phrase: “The spleen is a cemetery of erythrocytes”.

The process of autophagocytosis may be greatly intensified (this phenomenon is called hypersplenism ). Commonly, this is accompanied by enlargement of the spleen. The main manifestation of hypersplenism is panhemocytopenia, which includes hemolytic anemia, leukocytopenia and platelet deficit – due to sequestration and engulfment of these blood elements by splenic macrophages. Hypersplenism is common in some autoimmune disorders, like collagen vascular diseases, autoimmune trombocytopenic purpura (European synonym – Verlhoff’s disease) and in autoimmune anemias. The opposite state is hyposplenism.

The etiology of hyposplenism includes surgical removal of the spleen (most often – due to trauma of this organ and splenic hemorrhage, which is difficult to stop). It is provoked also by amyloidosis of the spleen and spleen fibrosis (in sickle cell anemia and autoimmune disorders). Thrombocytosis and visual signs of red blood cell aging accompany the hyposplenism.

The senescent erythrocytes are retained in bloodstream and carry basophilic Jolly-Howell bodies. The old WBC has multisegmented nucleus. The most unpleasant feature of hyposplenism and complication of splenectomy is, naturally, high risk of bacteriemia and parasitemia. For example, white rats develop exacerbation of bartonellosis after experimental splenectomy almost in 100% of cases. The spleen compasses about 25% of total lymphoid tissue in our body and has the huge army of macrophages. Several cytokines and leukokinins, produced by splenic cells, act locally for organization and augmentation of immune response. But they penetrate also in portal vein circulation, which gives to the splenic interleukins the shortest way to alter liver function and to start the hepatic production of acute phase proteins in preimmune response.

Taftsin, rigin and splenopoietin are increted from the spleen into portal vein and sometimes are referred to as “splenic hormones”. They stimulate phagocytosis. The spleen is also essential as a zone of high local cytokines concentration. The leukocytes circulate through it with bloodstream, so the local paracrine regulators obtain the unique possibility to act on numerous cells – like endocrine signals. All these aspects of splenic contribution into immunity make this organ quite important for immune system’s integrity. With great regret, we must remind that, for a long time, importance of spleen was underestimated by doctors, who have recommended splenectomy to many infectious patients – and sometimes not to their final benefit. In the beginning of XX century the famous Russian writer A.P. Tchekhov even wrote his novels under the pseudonym: “ A man without spleen” because he was splenectomized by his doctors due to tuberculosis, although it has produced untoward results.

At least, it is well-established fact that the splenectomy is commonly aggravated by lack of complement fragments in blood plasma, thus diminishing the ability of immune system to destroy the encapsulated microorganisms.

The clinically significant enlargement of the spleen is called splenomegaly.

Normal weight of the spleen in adults is not greater than 160 g and it is not palpable. In splenomegaly it gets palpable. The splenomegaly may have different reasons. Among them: venous congestion (e.g. portal hypertension syndrome), neoplastic and reactive lymphoid either histiocytic proliferations (e.g. chronic leukemias, histiocytosis X), inborn metabolic errors with macrophagal storage of some substances (e.g. Gauchet’s disease and Niemann-Pick’ disease), chronic infections (leishmaniasis, brucellosis), autoimmune disorders (systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, Verlhoff’s disease), extensive intravascular hemolysis (sickle cell anemia, microspherocytic anemia).

The immune function of the spleen is often deranged in splenomegaly. As a result, autoantibodies towards blood cells or liver may be registered in these patients. This can produce hemocytopenias or even Banti’s syndrome. Banti’s syndrome is described in 1894 as primary splenomegaly with subsequent: hypersplenism, anemia, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, hepatic cirrhosis and portal hypertension. Due to unknown, possibly infectious reason splenic lymphoid cells produce autoantibodies against cytoskeleton proteins. These autoantibodies cause damage of liver cells and intrahepatic biliary system and biliary fibrosis of the liver. Splenectomy is used for treatment of advanced cases.

 

The lymph nodes

The lymph nodes serve as filters in lymph circulation. The regional lymph nodes respond for the antigens brought via afferent lymph vessels.

In hematogenous dissemination of an antigen the reaction of the lymph nodes is generalized. An antigen goes into B-zone of the lymph node through afferent vessel. Within this zone it is processed and presented by local APC – dendritic reticular cells to B-lymphocytes. The last proliferate and form around APC the reactive germinal centers, looking very much like those of the spleen.

The paracortical zone of lymph node is T-dependent. Here the antigens are processed and presented to T-lymphocytes by local reticular cells.

The products of cell cooperation, sensitized T-lymphocytes and plasma cells are collected in medullar substance of the lymph node and recruited back into systemic circulation together with macrophages, via efferent lymphatic vessels and postcapillary HEV .

HEV or high endothelium venules – the type of venule specific for the immune system organs, distinguished by their high cubed endothelial cells, supplied with special system of surface adhesive receptor molecules – the addressins. The addressins belong to large family of cellular adhesion molecules – the selectins. The lymphocytes (and probably, some APC’s also) are able to recognize the addressins by means of their own surface adhesive receptor molecules – the integrins. The addressins recognition is responsible for homing of lymphoid cells, their recirculation between peripheral and central compartments of the immune system and other forms of lymphocyte addressed emigration from blood circulation.

So, the lymph node is a dynamic entity. The appearance of secondary germinal centers in lymph node witness for an immune response actually proceeding. The enlargement of a lymph node does not necessarily mean that a person has the lymphadenitis. A doctor needs to differentiate lymphadenitis and lymphadenopathy.

Lymphadenopathy is observed due to reactive hyperplasia of its lymphoid cells in course of the immune response. It is local (in inflammation) or systemic (in antigenemia, e.g. viremia). In these cases lymph node has normal cellular content, although the number and size of follicles is greater. The spectrum of autocoids in the node is represented with cytokines exclusively.

The enlargement of lymph nodes can be accompanied with the inflammation of the node itself and adjacent tissues. In cases like this the node contains some unusual cells immigrated from bloodstream and, quite often – also some infectious germs, persisting within the cells. This picture is referred to as lymphadenitis.

The lymphadenitis can be purulent (e.g. in staphylococci infection or any other germ easily destroyed by neutrophilic phagocytosis), granulomatous (in any infection by the germs able to survive within mononuclear phagocytes, like in tuberculosis), or combined pus-granulomatous (in cat’s scratch disease or in lymphogranuloma venereum).

Lymphadenopathy may present also in aseptic immunopathological disorders, like serum sickness and in local or systemic neoplastic diseases.

 

 

The last group includes, first of all, lymphomas, with Hodgkin’s disease among them. More rarely, histiocytic tumor or settlement of lymphoid leukemia blast cells within it causes lymph node enlargement.

According to some concepts APC of lymph nodes – reticular cell, give birth to the malignant clone of Berezovsky–Sternberg-Reed cells in Hodgkin’s lymphoma.

 

As a result of tumor embolism, the regional and remote lymph nodes may harbor the metastases of non-lymphoid malignant tumors.

The classic example of this phenomenon is Virchow’s metastasis of lung carcinoma into supraclavicular lymph nodes.

 

Thoracic Lymph Duct

The Thoracic Lymph Duct is a reach source of mature T-cells (and few B-cells also). The chronic drainage of this duct may cause the exhaustion of T-cells population and is utilized as a method of immune suppression.

Preface

Sacrilegiously we may compare the living organism to a programmed device because its adaptation is based on programmed responses. The repertoire of these responses (sometimes designated as reactivity) includes the entire diversity of individual genetic programs plus the epigenetic individual experience, superimposed over the genetic background and developed within genetically determined limits. Reactivity includes also some individual preference or tendency to select certain patterns of response from this available spectrum. We may say that reactivity of an organism is not only a library, but also a librarian. In a wide variety of situations, pathogenic signal starts some protective program, which is more or less universal and does not depend (or depends to a minor extent) on the character of the challenge. The examples of these non-specific defensive responses are thrombosis, inflammation, hyperemia etc. At the same time, even in the most primitive unicellular prokaryotic organisms one may observe the specific reactions, matched to the character of triggering stimulus. The most ancient example is, probably, an adaptive induction of enzyme biosynthesis, provoked by complementary specific substrates.

During the evolution living organisms developed a special system capable of creating and storing addressed protective programs individually picked up in relation to concrete pathogens. This system is based on inducible protein biosynthesis, and, if necessary, it has the property to unzip these programs from archive and to carry them out in the amplified (boosted) form.

This system has sensory functions as regards the molecular nature of self and alien biopolymers; it possesses the memory of the previous signals and learning capability.

 

By virtue of these features the repeated answers to the same pathogen differ from the initial one. The assemblage mentioned is the immune system.

 

 

Antigens

Living organism perseveres in the unfriendly environment in spite of mechanical, physical, chemical and biological pathogenic factors (pathogens) trying to harm it from without and a lot of unfavorable tendencies injuring it from within. The only way to survive and flourish is to permanently strain all its systems to achieve a balance between all kinds of damaging influences and the efforts of the organism to compensate thus maintaining homeostasis. The role of the immune system in achieving this goal is unrivaled, because the immune system sustains the homeostasis on the sub-cellular level being uniquely equipped with a mechanism to produce a myriad of special instruments, every of them specifically complimentary to one of a billion of various biopolymers molecule types. These molecules are called antigens and the specific instruments - antigen-specific lymphocyte receptors.

A substance is antigenic provided it has three-dimensional structure complicated enough for complementary key-lock principle interactions. In other words, it must be able to recognize and to be recognizable. Paul Ehrlich has formulated this principle in proverbial form: “ Corpora non agunt nisi fixata.” Antigenicity requires some degree of structural complexity and spatial rigidity. Even molecules with high molecular weight can be non-antigenic, having flat structure (cholesterol). The gelatin is high molecular weight protein, although devoid of tertiary structure and, as a result – non-antigenic (not able to form “key-hole”) (W. Boyd, 1969).

To coin a definition the antigens are biopolymeric molecules capable of inciting immune response in the organism by specifically binding to the lymphocyte antigen-specific receptors.

The antigens are a wide variety of natural and biosynthetic molecules sized between 1 and 10 kD (proteins, polysaccharides, complex esters and heterocyclic substances, nucleic acids, polynucleotides and their complexes, including lipid-containing ones).

The definition of antigen has been changed greatly through last 50 years. Initially it was regarded as simple synonym of “pathogen”, later immunologists re-interpreted this term as direct complementary cast of antibody, and finally it came to the definition mentioned above. Hence, antigens are polymeric molecules able to trigger specific immune response when introduced into organism. Interestingly, only polar molecules are highly immunogenic.

At the same time, highly spread popular interpretation of the word “antigen” as “alien protein” must be rejected from the very beginning of present discussion because of its anecdotal and outdated character. Moreover, many low molecular weight substances, like iodine, may generate unique three-dimensional complexes with biopolymeric carriers and provoke immune responses. They are called haptens.

An antigen is regarded by immunochemistry as individual one only if its components are adjoined by covalent chemical bounds. From this standpoint, cells, bacteria and multimolecular complexes possess manifold antigenic specificity. The minimal structural unit of an antigen, recognizable for antibodies and lymphocyte antigenic receptors is known as epitope or immunodominant region.

As a rule, epitopes are located in most mobile and configurable parts of quite rigid antigen structure. Each clone of lymphocytes is genetically programmed to recognize only one epitope, by means of an antigen- specific receptor on its surface, and hence these cells display exquisite antigen specificity. It is believed that billions of different epitopes may exist, consequently the equivalent number of lymphoid cell clones with their unique receptors is required for the recognition of this plenty.

The immune response requires certain density of epitopes: it disappears if their compactness is too high or too low. Single epitopes are non-immunogenic because for immune response the recognizing molecules must be cross-linked. Due to this overwhelming majority of immune responses needs cooperation of several specialized cells ( B-lymphocytes, T-lymphocytes, antigen-presenting cells ).

Some antigens include panels of numerous identical epitopes. Among them bacterial lipopolysaccharides, polymeric protein of Flagellates–flagellin, dextran, levan and other bacterial capsule polysaccharides, ficoll and polypeptides composed of D-aminoacids. They are hardly digestible for antigen-presenting cells, but able to cross-link the recognizing lymphocyte receptors directly. This group of antigens provokes polyclonal B-cell activation and immunoglobulin production without T-cell cooperation. Many individual antigens have several or even numerous different epitopes and elicit immune response of various cellular clones, quite often simultaneously in different forms (both antibody secretion and clonal expansion of cellular effectors).

All antigens may be formally classified into exogenous and endogenous depending on their origin. This distinction is relative for it depends on whether you look at the antigens from within this or that organism. Therefore the endogenous and exogenous antigens do not differ in shape, nature, size, smell or taste. Even the attitude of the immune system almost does not depend on whether the antigen be endogenous or exogenous, e.g., exogenous antigens of transfused donor red blood cells are tolerated, whereas the endogenous antigens arising on the recipients' own olden erythrocytes incite a cold-blooded murder of these battered veterans by the immune system. To be more exact, antigen-presenting cell process differently the antigens, coming from within the cell itself and those engulfed from without. But, it does not matter if the endocellular antigen is self or, for example, viral and if extracellular one is bacterial, or belongs to the organism's own senescent cell.

Of much greater significance for immune system is a classification categorizing all antigens into autoantigens (self) and heteroantigens (non-self). Endoantigens are not necessarily autoantigens. The autoantigens are the biopolymers characteristic of youthful normal unimpaired cells; although these molecules are superabundant, they normally produce no other response but immunologic tolerance. The markers of senescence, expressed on aged cells and pathologic neoantigens, induced by viral infection, trauma, and oncogenesis - do not belong to autoantigens. All these antigens are included in modified-self group and treated by immune system as alien ones. The heteroantigens are, on the other hand, the molecules that can not be found in youthful normal unimpaired cells, on the contrary, they appear in sick, olden, injured or tumor cells, as well as alien ones, or invade the organism from without. These heteroantigens are not tolerated normally, but rather produce full-fledged immune response. Without a doubt, the heteroantigens may be of exogenous as well as endogenous origin (neoantigens and modified-self ones). On the other hand there are both endogenous autoantigens (which is only natural), and exogenous (cross-reactive) ones.

It takes all sorts of antigens to make the immune system work properly for they are the major objet it manipulates.

In fact, antigen is not only material molecule, but also information unit or signal.

As any kind of new information, antigens need presentation for broad recognition and acknowledgement.

According to the classic concept, antigens display the epitopes of different types. Conformation (discontinuous) determinants represent the forms created by tertiary structure of biopolymers. For example, in protein antigens it may be represented by amino acid residues located far from each other in primary polypeptide chain, but put together in spatial three-dimensional globes of fibrils. As a rule, they protrude out of the whole antigen molecule. Sequential (continuous) determinants are all but simple and short linear sequences produced by primary structure, for example short peptides of  8-9 to 20-28 amino acids in protein molecule.

In course of immune response for extracellular antigens, during antigen processing after phagocytosis, conformation epitopes are lost, but sequential are exposed and presented by antigen-presenting cells on their surface, as a rind of panel. In course of immune response for intracellular antigens, sequential determinants are created in translation and presented by antigen-presenting cells before complete set of conformation determinants is acquired in post-translation modifications.

 

The Elements of Immune System

The immune system (immunitas in Latin means freedom (from disease)) is a source of complex and highly sophisticated defense strategies that is often subdivided into two major categories, innate and adaptive, each endowed with a humoral and cellular arm. The immune system consists of the numerous solid and dispersed circulating elements, with total mass in adult person of approximately 1,5 kg (Fig.1). The central organs of immunogenesis are those, where immature lymphocytes develop and are exposed to primary clonal selection.

In humans these are bone marrow and thymus. The peripheral organs, where mature lymphocytes live, undergo antigen-dependant clonal expansion and carry out the immune responses, include, besides spleen and lymph nodes, also Waldeier-Pirogov lymphoepithelial pharyngeal ring and diffuse non-encapsulated lymphoid elements of gastrointestinal, bronchial and urogenital mucosa.

The bone marrow carries out both functions of central and peripheral lymphoid organ, being a place of clonal differentiation as well as clonal expansion. The blood is also part of immune system, as the elements of this system, both specific, and non- specific, have the ability to circulate, which is essential for their function. It concerns to lymphoid cells, immunoglobulins (Ig), complement proteins and another effectors of the immune response. For this reason, local sensitization can proceed in system sensitivity.

Interaction of specific cellular elements (lymphocytes) and their products (antibodies and cytokines) provide the optimum performance of immune system. They interact with each other, and also with non-lymphoid elements. Majors of the last ones are antigen presenting cells (APC).

Immune system becomes more and more complicated in a course of evolution. The recognition of alien entities is traced in Spongia, and alien rejection is observed since Hydrae. In Anthoza immunologic memory and cytotoxicity are described. Since lowest Vertebrates there is an interaction of APC and effector cells of immunity (E.L. Cooper, 1979). Some Annelid worms have leukocytes, capable to antigen-induced proliferation. The precise division on Т- and B- lymphocytes is revealed in Fishes. The antibodies such as IgM occur in Cyclostomata. The Amphibians have IgG, and the Bird - also IgA. The fishes have thymus and spleen, non-caudate Amphibians form lymph nodes and bone marrow, and Birds - Fabricius bursa. Finally, only and exclusively supreme placentate Mammals produce IgE (М. J. Manning, 1979).

The innate resistance system may be viewed as the first line of defense. Its cellular components, are ideally suited for this role because their actions do not depend on prior sensitization or "priming" by the antigens present on their target cells (e.g., microbes, tumor cells) and furthermore they do not possess the fine antigen specificity that is characteristic of the adaptive (or specific) immune responses. Thus, for example, a given macrophage can whet its appetite by ingesting a wide variety of antigens. Complement system is the major humoral component of innate immunity. Innate immunity causes so called pre-immune response or acute phase response after immediately invasion of any infect or non-infectious antigen. Innate non-specific mechanisms are embedded into the building of specific immune response as very essential bricks or even milestones. Due to opportunistic nature of evolution (R. Gullielmin, 1991), archetypes or old defensive programs, once discovered by evolution, are not abandoned after appearance of newer ones, but updated and used for the purposes established by new programs. The phagocytosis appeared in unicellular organisms, long before immune system rise, serves for the aims of antigen processing and presentation. Complement and other ancient effectors of inflammation are enhanced and specifically targeted by younger warrior of evolution - immune response. The principal cellular components of the adaptive immune responses are T and B-lymphocytes.

As a rule, primary immune response for an antigen modifies the immune system. Repeated penetration of the same antigen results in boosted secondary immune response. This phenomenon traditionally is called by figurative name “positive immunologic memory”. The memory is antigen-specific, unlike so-called anamnestic response, last meaning just non-specific increase in immunogenesis by any antigen against different ones due to universal production of immunostimulant cytokines. Primary non-boosted immune response gives noticeable titers of circulating antibodies 1-2 weeks after first contact with an antigen. Maximal antibody production is achieved 1-2 months later, than their concentration declines. IgM are initially predominant in primary response, with later rise in Ig G titers. If the antigen is already known to organism, repeated exposure produce more rapid and strong response, with noticeable titer of specific antibodies achieved as early as in few days, with much higher maximal level, remaining stable for many months. Ig G predominates from the very beginning of secondary immune response. In some cases first contact with an antigen changes the immune system in such a way, that repeated stimulation causes no response or produces decreases response. This is the situation sometimes mentioned as negative immunologic memory or acquired immunologic tolerance.

The main criteria of actual immunologic reactions are specificity and immunologic memory. A cell recognizes glucose or aspirin by means of specific target molecules (receptors), but this does not cause immunologic memory for such substrates, consequently similar reactions are not considered to be immunologic.

The term “memory cell” is very popular in immunologic literature, but it is necessary to remind that it is just allegory. There are no cells which “remember only doing nothing else”. The fable word “memory” is used just to stress the ability for enhanced boosted immune response – and nothing more exotic. It means that all T- and B-lymphocyte appeared after primary immune response as a result of clonal selection and antigen-induced clonal expansion process are real memory cells.

They are able to respond in enhanced manner after contact with specific antigen, and their B-subset starts antibody production immediately with IgG, unlike their ancestors, beginning with IgM.

The majority of antigens are not able to cross-link the lymphocyte receptors. Due to this, regulatory T-cells can respond only if an antigen is presented by APC, as a panel of sequential determinants, in association with the glycoproteins of major histocompatibility complex (MHC).

 

B-cells do recognize conformation epitopes of the intact antigen and, principally, are capable to respond autonomously for the epitopes, complementary to their surface immunoglobulin receptors. But they need T cell help for full-fledged complete immune response. Moreover, many antigens in rough natural form induce tolerance of B-cells on direct contact.

The general features of the immune system can be discussed as follows.

During evolution, humans have developed the ability to recognize myriad of foreign antigens and hence a normal individual must possess antigen-specific cells that can react with a staggering number of antigens. Because the human body is not merely a bad of lymphocytes (as many immunologists believe!), it follows that in unprimed individuals the number of lymphocytes that can react to any given specific antigen must be extremely small. However, when challenged with an antigen, say in the form of old, sick, damaged or tumor cells, microbial infection, antigen-specific lymphocytes divide and expand to effectively handle the unwelcome inmate. Cellular components of immune system, both specific, and not specific, can proliferate. The multiplication of lymphocytes occurs not only in the central immune organs: the secondary duplication of stimulated cells takes place also peripherally. This feature allows considerable increasing of the immune reactions, but also requires controlling and braking mechanisms for prevention of the accidental or excessive answers. All examined immunologic reactions are under the control and represent intermingling balanced network of stimulatory and suppressing signals.

Such clonal expansions of T and B lymphocytes is a complex multi-step process involving both differentiation and cell division; therefore, it takes at least a few days, sometimes weeks, before the process is completed. Because of this requirement, specific immune responses are better suited as the second line of defense, which eventually becomes the major protective mechanism.

Although the distinctions between innate and induced immunity and the cellular and humoral effector systems are helpful resolving the complexity of the host defense systems, it is extremely important to remember that there are multiple interconnections and "cross talk" among the various components. Recall that the complement cascade, an important nonspecific humoral component, can be triggered directly by an invading organism (the alternate pathway) or by antibodies formed in the course of a specific immune response. Complement, in turn, plays an important role in modulating the function of neutrophils and macrophages, as detailed earlier. Macrophages are not only important effector cells in the expression of innate immunity, but also, as antigen-presenting cells, they play an important role in triggering specific immune responses mediated by B and T lymphocytes. The latter, as is well known, mediate function of antibody-producing B cells as well as that of macrophages and natural killer (NK) cells. The physiologic significance of these interactions is often evident when a genetic or acquired disease selectively destroys just one component of this finely tuned "orchestra", as is exemplified by the acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS). In this disorder, the destruction of a subset of T cells by the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) produces a cascade of effects that eventually cripple almost the entire host defense apparatus.

Although vital to survival, the immune system is like the proverbial two-edged sword. On the one hand, immunodeficiency states render humans easy prey to infections and possibly tumors; on the other hand, a hyperactive immune system may cause fatal disease, as in the case of an overwhelming allergic reaction to the sting of a bee. In yet another series of derangement, the immune system may lose its normal capacity to distinguish self from non-self, resulting in the emergence of immunity against one's own tissues and cells (autoumminity). In this chapter, we consider diseases caused by too little immunity as well as those resulting from too much immunologic reactivity. But first, we review some recent advances in the understanding of lymphocyte biology, to be followed by a brief description of the histocompatibility genes, because their products are relevant to several immunologically mediated diseases and to the rejection of transplants.

Lymphoid Organs

The discussion of immune defense benefits and costs shall be started with the description of lymphoid organs’ structure and histophysiology.

After birth lymphocytes are, mostly, generated in central lymphoid organs: thymus and bone marrow, which are responsible for the development of their clonal diversity and tolerance (these processes together are called priming), although clonal expansion may occur also in peripheral lymphoid tissue.

The Bone Marrow

The Bone Marrow, which is, in fact, both central and peripheral lymphoid organ shall be discussed first. The progenitors of B lymphocytes develop in humans within yolk sac and fetal liver, after birth this [process concentrates in bone marrow. In postnatal period bone marrow produces both T- and B-cells from common lymphoid progenitor cell. B-cells are produced in this compartment of immune system through all life long. It is much more complex process than simple proliferation. B-cell differentiation suggests active somatic mutation and alternative splicing of the genes, encoding the variants of different immunoglobulin portions. This creates the clonal diversity of B-lymphocytes, which leave bone marrow differentiated enough.

Unlike B-cells, T-lymphocytes precursors, produced within bone marrow and diversified as a set of clones with different structure of surface antigenic receptor, are still not mature and transmigrate into thymus for subsequent differentiation with parallel positive and negative clonal selection or priming.

As a peripheral lymphoid organ, bone marrow contains, along with hematopoetic progenitors, also mature B and T-cells, macrophages and their ancestors. If progenitor cell perish within bone marrow (due to: irradiation, exposure to chemical mutagens, viruses, expansion and competition of malignant atypical cells, autoimmune cytolytic or cytostatic effect) patient may suffer from lymphopenia and immunodeficiency. Constant gene recombination within lymphoid progenitor cells creates relatively high risk of somatic mutation provoking expression of oncogenes. As a result of it, atypical lymphoid blasts may arise, causing lymphogenous leukemia or lymphomas. These types of malignancies, according P. Chandrasoma and C.R. Taylor (1998), totally occupy sixth place in incidence among neoplasias in population of North America. Quite frequently aplastic and neoplastic processes of lymphoid clones are combined within bone marrow of the same patient.

 

 

 

The Thymus

Thymic Gland or Thymus: is an organ of rather complex structure, which was believed to be the “Seat of the Soul and Courage” by Ruphus of Ephesus (100 B.C.) and also by ancient Hindu wisemen. It has retrosternal location and consists of two lobes and many lobules. Each of them contains cortical and medullar substances (M.D. Kendall, 1981). Tightly packed small T-lymphocytes are predominant in thymus cortex. There are also epithelial nurse-cells.

The medullar substance, besides T-cells (more mature and less tightly packed than in the cortex, supplied with CD44 marker), has also the mesh of epithelial cells, attached to stroma and contacting with lymphocytes. The specific dense structures formed by knot of medullar epithelial cells, degenerated in apoptosis, is called Hassall’s corpuscles. Thymic stroma and stellate or D-cells (APC analogues) are derived from 3rd and 4th pharyngeal pouches. These cells present MHC glycoproteins to local maturing thymocytes (lymphoid T-cells), which came from yolk sac, fetal liver and bone marrow and settled within thymus. MHC antigen presentation is necessary for priming or development of MHC restriction.

The intensive mitoses of lymphocytes and programmed death of T-cells both determine the definite set of mature T-clones, designated to leave thymus and settle within peripheral lymphoid organs.

Not all, but few T-cells, enrolled at Thymic School will graduate from it. After complete course of “education” the survived graduates, able to interact with autologious cells of immune system, but tolerant to autoantigens leave thymus via venules. For a long time crucial role of thymus in immunogenesis was not appreciated. As late as in 1963, such an authoritative immunologist as Noble Prize winner (1960), discoverer of acquired immunologic tolerance, P. Medavar still wrote sincerely and mistakenly: “ we shall come to regard the presence of lymphocytes in the thymus as an evolutionary accident of no very great significance”. Yet, in 1961-1962 J.F.A.P. Miller from Australia performed the decisive experiments, allowed him to conclude that: “ during embryogenesis the thymus would produce the originators of immunologically competent cells, many of which would have migrated to other sites at about the time of birth. This would suggest that lymphocytes, leaving the thymus are specially selected cells”. Later (1967-1968) the same author together with J.F. Mitchell have demonstrated that lymphocytes come into thymus from bone marrow and are educated there by means of positive and negative selection mechanisms.

The defects in thymus embryogenesis cause the disorder of T-cell maturing resulting in immunodeficiency.

The examples are complete aplasia of thymus – Di George’s syndrome and thymic dysplasia – Nezeloff’s syndrome.

Thymic disorders alter the process of autoreactive clones negative selection and thus provoke autoimmune diseases.

 

The thymus is subject of age-dependent accidental involution, which probably causes some decrease of immune surveillance and violations of self-tolerance in elderly individuals.

The stress accelerates the rate of thymic involution and triggers the apoptosis in thymocytes.

 

 

Thymus is not only immune, but also endocrine organ (Z. Kemileva, 1984). Several polypeptide hormones are produced by epithelial thymic cells and increted into blood. Thymopoietin (also known as thymosin) facilitates the maturing of T-cells and displays corticoliberin-like activity. Thymulin is another thymic hormone. It is Zn-containing peptide, essential for immunogenesis. That’s why, zinc deficient diet is dangerous for decrease of immunity (J. -F. Bach, 1984). Besides these true hormones, epithelial thymic cells, T-lymphocytes of thymus and migrant macrophages and APC secrete a lot of other cytokines.

Thymic aplasia is characteristic for deficit of T-cell cytokines, especially IL-2, and therefore in these patients both cellular and humoral immune reactions may be deranged.

Hyperplasia of thymus is associated with peculiar change of reactivity – status thymico-lymphaticus commonly listed in classic medical texts among so called diatheses. Thymico-lymphatic status is characteristic for thymus hyperplasia and delay of it’s age-related atrophy. Peripheral lymphoid organs also are somewhat enlarged. This is accompanied by hypoplasia of adrenals and even tendency to hypofunction of these endocrine glands. It is believed that ability to stress response is decreased in these individuals. In accordance with classic concept, there is some relation between status thymico-lymphaticus and increased risk of sudden infant’s death syndrome, although most recent researches did not witness for such correlation.

From the immunological point of view, there is one very essential detail: in thymus hyperplasia B-lymphoid follicles appear within thymus, although normally B-cells are absent in thymic gland. The persons with thymic hyperplasia often display some disorders of self-tolerance. The bright example is high incidence of myasthenia gravis with autoimmunity towards N-cholinergic receptor among the individuals having thymic hyperplasia or thymic epithelial malignant tumor – thymoma.

Thymoma is commonly accompanied by some of the long spectrum of autoimmune disorders (aplastic anemia, systemic lupus erythematosus, and autoimmune endocrinopathies). Thymus is involved also in T-cell lymphoblastic leukemia and in T-lymphomas, which as a rule are metastatic (from bone marrow) and quite rarely originate locally, within thymus.

 

 

Having discussed the main features of the central lymphoid organs, we shall proceed to the peripheral ones.

The peripheral lymphoid organs and clusters of lymphocytes contain mature lymphocytes and accessory APC as well. They are distributed far and wide through the organism. Immunocompetent cells present even behind the blood brain barrier (intrathecal lymphocytes and microglial cells of CNS). However, there exists a strong tendency for lymphoid tissue to occur predominantly at portals of antigenic entry. The relative percentage of T and B-cells varies in peripheral compartments of immune system, depending on location (see table 1 below).

The Spleen

The Spleen works as the lymphoid filter in blood circulation system. This is an important seat of antibody production, generally in response to circulating cellular and bacterial antigens, brought with blood flow. The lymphocytes also came into spleen with blood flow. The spleen consists of the capsule and trabecules or cords, protruding into its parenchyma. Capsule and cords support the red pulp and white pulp. The red pulp, mostly, consists of macrophages and other reticuloendothelial elements, surrounding the venous sinusoids. The sparse plasma cells (B-lymphocytes, producing antibodies) are scattered among them. The white pulp is presented with lymphoid tissue, forming nodules around the arteries and arterioles. The penicillary (in European texts – central) arteries branch from the trabecular arteries and ramify within the lymphoid nodules (in classic texts – Malpighian corpuscles). The periarteriolar muffs or sheaths envelop the central arterioles, diverging from penicillary arteries. These sheaths are stuffed with the alumni of Thymus School – T-lymphocytes. B-lymphocytes settle in another ghetto of an organ – they form primary follicles and, in course of immune responses - also secondary follicles with germinal centers, both types of B-follicles are located laterally from T-dependent zones. The follicles contain the APC for B-cells (dendritic cells).

The spleen is the major arena of gladiatorial combats for destroy of the microbial and self opsonized cells, for example sepsis agents or damaged and senescent blood cells. This process occurs within red pulp. For sure, there is no medical student who is not familiar with the sacramental phrase: “The spleen is a cemetery of erythrocytes”.

The process of autophagocytosis may be greatly intensified (this phenomenon is called hypersplenism ). Commonly, this is accompanied by enlargement of the spleen. The main manifestation of hypersplenism is panhemocytopenia, which includes hemolytic anemia, leukocytopenia and platelet deficit – due to sequestration and engulfment of these blood elements by splenic macrophages. Hypersplenism is common in some autoimmune disorders, like collagen vascular diseases, autoimmune trombocytopenic purpura (European synonym – Verlhoff’s disease) and in autoimmune anemias. The opposite state is hyposplenism.

The etiology of hyposplenism includes surgical removal of the spleen (most often – due to trauma of this organ and splenic hemorrhage, which is difficult to stop). It is provoked also by amyloidosis of the spleen and spleen fibrosis (in sickle cell anemia and autoimmune disorders). Thrombocytosis and visual signs of red blood cell aging accompany the hyposplenism.

The senescent erythrocytes are retained in bloodstream and carry basophilic Jolly-Howell bodies. The old WBC has multisegmented nucleus. The most unpleasant feature of hyposplenism and complication of splenectomy is, naturally, high risk of bacteriemia and parasitemia. For example, white rats develop exacerbation of bartonellosis after experimental splenectomy almost in 100% of cases. The spleen compasses about 25% of total lymphoid tissue in our body and has the huge army of macrophages. Several cytokines and leukokinins, produced by splenic cells, act locally for organization and augmentation of immune response. But they penetrate also in portal vein circulation, which gives to the splenic interleukins the shortest way to alter liver function and to start the hepatic production of acute phase proteins in preimmune response.

Taftsin, rigin and splenopoietin are increted from the spleen into portal vein and sometimes are referred to as “splenic hormones”. They stimulate phagocytosis. The spleen is also essential as a zone of high local cytokines concentration. The leukocytes circulate through it with bloodstream, so the local paracrine regulators obtain the unique possibility to act on numerous cells – like endocrine signals. All these aspects of splenic contribution into immunity make this organ quite important for immune system’s integrity. With great regret, we must remind that, for a long time, importance of spleen was underestimated by doctors, who have recommended splenectomy to many infectious patients – and sometimes not to their final benefit. In the beginning of XX century the famous Russian writer A.P. Tchekhov even wrote his novels under the pseudonym: “ A man without spleen” because he was splenectomized by his doctors due to tuberculosis, although it has produced untoward results.

At least, it is well-established fact that the splenectomy is commonly aggravated by lack of complement fragments in blood plasma, thus diminishing the ability of immune system to destroy the encapsulated microorganisms.

The clinically significant enlargement of the spleen is called splenomegaly.

Normal weight of the spleen in adults is not greater than 160 g and it is not palpable. In splenomegaly it gets palpable. The splenomegaly may have different reasons. Among them: venous congestion (e.g. portal hypertension syndrome), neoplastic and reactive lymphoid either histiocytic proliferations (e.g. chronic leukemias, histiocytosis X), inborn metabolic errors with macrophagal storage of some substances (e.g. Gauchet’s disease and Niemann-Pick’ disease), chronic infections (leishmaniasis, brucellosis), autoimmune disorders (systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, Verlhoff’s disease), extensive intravascular hemolysis (sickle cell anemia, microspherocytic anemia).

The immune function of the spleen is often deranged in splenomegaly. As a result, autoantibodies towards blood cells or liver may be registered in these patients. This can produce hemocytopenias or even Banti’s syndrome. Banti’s syndrome is described in 1894 as primary splenomegaly with subsequent: hypersplenism, anemia, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, hepatic cirrhosis and portal hypertension. Due to unknown, possibly infectious reason splenic lymphoid cells produce autoantibodies against cytoskeleton proteins. These autoantibodies cause damage of liver cells and intrahepatic biliary system and biliary fibrosis of the liver. Splenectomy is used for treatment of advanced cases.

 

The lymph nodes

The lymph nodes serve as filters in lymph circulation. The regional lymph nodes respond for the antigens brought via afferent lymph vessels.

In hematogenous dissemination of an antigen the reaction of the lymph nodes is generalized. An antigen goes into B-zone of the lymph node through afferent vessel. Within this zone it is processed and presented by local APC – dendritic reticular cells to B-lymphocytes. The last proliferate and form around APC the reactive germinal centers, looking very much like those of the spleen.

The paracortical zone of lymph node is T-dependent. Here the antigens are processed and presented to T-lymphocytes by local reticular cells.

The products of cell cooperation, sensitized T-lymphocytes and plasma cells are collected in medullar substance of the lymph node and recruited back into systemic circulation together with macrophages, via efferent lymphatic vessels and postcapillary HEV .

HEV or high endothelium venules – the type of venule specific for the immune system organs, distinguished by their high cubed endothelial cells, supplied with special system of surface adhesive receptor molecules – the addressins. The addressins belong to large family of cellular adhesion molecules – the selectins. The lymphocytes (and probably, some APC’s also) are able to recognize the addressins by means of their own surface adhesive receptor molecules – the integrins. The addressins recognition is responsible for homing of lymphoid cells, their recirculation between peripheral and central compartments of the immune system and other forms of lymphocyte addressed emigration from blood circulation.

So, the lymph node is a dynamic entity. The appearance of secondary germinal centers in lymph node witness for an immune response actually proceeding. The enlargement of a lymph node does not necessarily mean that a person has the lymphadenitis. A doctor needs to differentiate lymphadenitis and lymphadenopathy.

Lymphadenopathy is observed due to reactive hyperplasia of its lymphoid cells in course of the immune response. It is local (in inflammation) or systemic (in antigenemia, e.g. viremia). In these cases lymph node has normal cellular content, although the number and size of follicles is greater. The spectrum of autocoids in the node is represented with cytokines exclusively.

The enlargement of lymph nodes can be accompanied with the inflammation of the node itself and adjacent tissues. In cases like this the node contains some unusual cells immigrated from bloodstream and, quite often – also some infectious germs, persisting within the cells. This picture is referred to as lymphadenitis.

The lymphadenitis can be purulent (e.g. in staphylococci infection or any other germ easily destroyed by neutrophilic phagocytosis), granulomatous (in any infection by the germs able to survive within mononuclear phagocytes, like in tuberculosis), or combined pus-granulomatous (in cat’s scratch disease or in lymphogranuloma venereum).

Lymphadenopathy may present also in aseptic immunopathological disorders, like serum sickness and in local or systemic neoplastic diseases.

 

 

The last group includes, first of all, lymphomas, with Hodgkin’s disease among them. More rarely, histiocytic tumor or settlement of lymphoid leukemia blast cells within it causes lymph node enlargement.

According to some concepts APC of lymph nodes – reticular cell, give birth to the malignant clone of Berezovsky–Sternberg-Reed cells in Hodgkin’s lymphoma.

 

As a result of tumor embolism, the regional and remote lymph nodes may harbor the metastases of non-lymphoid malignant tumors.

The classic example of this phenomenon is Virchow’s metastasis of lung carcinoma into supraclavicular lymph nodes.

 

Thoracic Lymph Duct

The Thoracic Lymph Duct is a reach source of mature T-cells (and few B-cells also). The chronic drainage of this duct may cause the exhaustion of T-cells population and is utilized as a method of immune suppression.
